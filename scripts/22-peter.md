# Episode 22 — Peter

**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #8 (Confession→A→B(short)), Slot A #4 / Slot B #12
**Thesis:** Peter is the only apostle who failed publicly, repeatedly, and on the record — swore he didn't know the man, was rebuked as Satan to his face, sank in the water he'd asked to walk on — and was still the one handed the keys, given the commission, and told three times to feed the sheep.
**Epistemic honesty:** The Gospels preserve more of Peter's failures than any other disciple's; whether that reflects his actual prominence or editorial choice is noted but not resolved here.

I'll be honest — I put this one off. Not because Peter is obscure. Because he isn't. He's possibly the most written-about apostle in history, and the version most people carry around in their heads is either the triumphant rock of the church or the cautionary tale who denied Christ three times. Both of those are real, and both are incomplete, and the distance between them is where the actual person lives.

I'm Naomi — I don't do the highlight reel. I do the person.

[narration]

Peter's given name is Simon. He's a fisherman from Bethsaida, working on the Sea of Galilee with his brother Andrew. Jesus renames him — Cephas in Aramaic, Petros in Greek, both meaning rock. The renaming happens before he's done anything to earn it. That's worth noting. The name is prophetic, not descriptive — it points to something that hasn't happened yet.

What the Gospels record about Peter is remarkable in its honesty. He's the one who walks on water — and sinks. Matthew 14: he sees Jesus on the lake, asks to come out, steps onto the surface, looks at the wind, and goes under. Jesus catches him. "You of little faith, why did you doubt?" The story preserves both the courage to step out and the failure to sustain it. Most people remember the sinking. Fewer note that he was the only one who got out of the boat at all.

At Caesarea Philippi, he makes the confession — "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God" — and Jesus responds with the keys of the kingdom, the authority to bind and loose. Five verses later, Peter rebukes Jesus for predicting his own death, and Jesus turns and says to him: "Get behind me, Satan." The distance between those two moments is five verses. The highest commendation and the sharpest rebuke in the Gospels, directed at the same man, in the same conversation.

Then the denial. Luke 22: Jesus tells Peter directly that Satan has asked to sift him like wheat, that Peter will deny him three times before the rooster crows that night. Peter insists he won't. He does. Three times — to a servant girl, to a bystander, to another questioner. The third time, the rooster crows, and Luke adds a detail the other Gospels don't: "The Lord turned and looked at Peter." That's all. A look. Peter goes out and weeps bitterly.

[dramatic]

The restoration is in John 21. After the resurrection, Peter has gone back to fishing. Jesus appears on the shore, cooks breakfast, and asks Peter three times: "Do you love me?" Three questions, matching three denials. Peter is grieved by the third time — the text says so. Each time Jesus responds the same way: "Feed my lambs. Tend my sheep. Feed my sheep." The commission isn't withdrawn. It's re-given, three times, in a structure that mirrors exactly what broke.

In Acts, Peter is transformed. He preaches at Pentecost — Acts 2 — and three thousand people respond. He heals at the temple gate. He stands before the Sanhedrin without flinching. He receives the vision in Acts 10 that opens the movement to Gentiles — Cornelius's household, the sheet coming down from heaven with unclean animals, the voice saying "Do not call anything impure that God has made clean." Peter resists three times before he obeys. Even his obedience has the same stubbornness his failures did.

And then Galatians 2: Paul confronts him publicly in Antioch because Peter stopped eating with Gentile believers when certain men came from James. Even after the Cornelius episode, even after the Jerusalem Council, Peter withdrew. Paul opposed him to his face. The text preserves this without softening it.

That's the whole shape of him. A man who confessed and denied, who walked on water and sank, who was called rock and called Satan, who was told to feed the sheep and then pulled away from the Gentile table when it cost him socially. The record doesn't choose one version. It holds all of them. If you want more people the text gives you whole, contradictions included — that's what we're doing here. I'll see you in the next one.
