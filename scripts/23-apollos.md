Apollos. A man shows up in Ephesus — a Jew from Alexandria, which in the first century meant one of the great intellectual centers of the Mediterranean world, a city with a Jewish community large enough to have produced the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures. He's described in Acts 18 as learned — the Greek word is logios, which carries weight: eloquent, educated, rhetorically trained. He's fervent in spirit. He speaks boldly in the synagogue. He teaches accurately about Jesus. And he knows only the baptism of John.

I'm Naomi. This channel exists for exactly one thing: the people history either got wrong or skipped entirely.

[narration]

The detail about John's baptism is the pivot of the whole account. Acts 18:25 says Apollos taught accurately the things concerning Jesus, but knew only the baptism of John. What that means precisely has generated centuries of scholarly discussion. The simplest reading: he knew the preparatory message — repentance, the coming Messiah — but not the full apostolic teaching about the death, resurrection, and the Holy Spirit. He had the architecture without the capstone. And he was teaching it publicly, with force, and people were responding.

A married couple heard him in the synagogue. Priscilla and Aquila — tentmakers, Paul's co-workers, already established in Ephesus. Acts 18:26 says they took him aside and explained the way of God to him more accurately. The Greek verb is proslambanō — they took him to themselves, privately. Not a public correction. Not a rebuke. They heard a brilliant man teaching incomplete truth and chose to fill the gap without making a scene. The text gives Priscilla's name first, which in the conventions of the period usually signals prominence.

What happened after is equally telling. Apollos wanted to cross to Achaia — to Corinth, specifically. The believers in Ephesus wrote a letter encouraging the disciples there to welcome him. He arrived and, Acts 18:28 says, "greatly helped those who through grace had believed, for he powerfully refuted the Jews in public, showing by the Scriptures that the Christ was Jesus." The word translated "powerfully" is eutonōs — with full force, vigorously. The man who'd been quietly corrected in someone's home went on to become one of the most effective public advocates in the early movement.

[sigh]

Then comes Corinth's problem. First Corinthians 1:12 — Paul writes that the church has divided itself into factions: "I follow Paul," "I follow Apollos," "I follow Cephas," "I follow Christ." Apollos had become, without apparently intending it, a brand. His rhetorical power — the Alexandrian education, the ability to demolish arguments publicly — created a following. Paul spends chapter 3 addressing this directly: "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth." He insists they're co-laborers, not competitors. The one who planted and the one who watered are nothing — the field belongs to God.

What Paul says about Apollos in those passages is entirely positive. There is no rivalry in the text, despite the factions forming around their names. In 1 Corinthians 16:12, Paul mentions that he strongly urged Apollos to visit Corinth again, but Apollos was not willing to go just then — the phrasing suggests Apollos made his own decisions about timing, and Paul respected it. Titus 3:13 asks that Apollos be sent on his way with everything he needs. The relationship appears functional and warm.

What's left in the record, then, is a man who was brilliant and incomplete, who was corrected by people with less formal education than he had and received it, who became powerful enough to split a church without trying, and who appears in the letters as someone Paul trusted and treated as a peer. The correction didn't diminish him. The factions weren't his fault. The text holds all of it together without needing to resolve the tension.
