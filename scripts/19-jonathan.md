# Episode 19 — Jonathan
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #4 (A+C folded), Slot A #8 / Slot C folded
**Thesis:** Jonathan, crown prince of Israel, knew exactly who David was and what it meant — gave him his armor, his weapons, and his loyalty anyway, and died before any of it resolved.
**Grounded in:** 1 Samuel 13–31, 2 Samuel 1
**Tags used:** [whispering]

---

There is a scene — early in the story, before anyone knows how it ends — where the crown prince of Israel meets a young man who has just killed a giant, strips off his own armor, takes off his robe, unstraps his sword and his bow, unbuckles his belt, and hands all of it over. Every piece of what a prince wears to signal who he is. Every physical marker of his rank. He gives it to someone he met hours ago.

The text says he loved him as his own soul.

Naomi here — and we're spending this one on Jonathan.

[narration]

Jonathan is Saul's oldest son, which in the world of 1 Samuel means he is the presumptive next king of Israel. This is not a minor detail. The monarchy is new — Saul is the first — and the expectation of inheritance is real. Jonathan has already demonstrated military capability before David enters the story: he leads a raid on a Philistine garrison at Michmash with only his armor-bearer and apparently without telling his father, and the resulting panic among the Philistines is described in detail. He is, by the account, a fighter and a leader in his own right.

The covenant he makes with David in 1 Samuel 18 comes immediately after the Goliath episode. Jonathan strips himself of the symbols of his position and transfers them. Scholars read this variously — as a gesture of friendship, as a kind of symbolic anointing, as an acknowledgment of something Jonathan already understood about where David was headed. The text doesn't clarify which it is. It records the action and the phrase about love and moves on.

What follows is a series of episodes in which Jonathan places himself, repeatedly, between his father and David. When Saul first speaks openly about killing David, Jonathan goes to David and tells him — then goes to Saul and argues the case, and temporarily succeeds. When Saul's suspicion returns and hardened, Jonathan and David arrange a signal: Jonathan will shoot arrows in a field, and the direction he sends the boy to retrieve them will tell David whether it's safe to come back or time to run. He shoots the arrows far. He sends the boy away. And then — [whispering] and there is no boy in the account at that point — the two of them say goodbye in private.

The final meeting recorded between them is at Horesh, while David is hiding from Saul in the wilderness. Jonathan goes to him, which means he actively sought him out, in a place Saul was also trying to find. The line Jonathan speaks in 1 Samuel 23:17 is one of the most striking in these narratives: "Do not be afraid, for the hand of my father Saul shall not find you. You shall be king over Israel, and I shall be second to you." He says this directly. He appears to believe it. He apparently views this as an acceptable arrangement.

What Jonathan didn't know — or maybe did know and accepted — was that he wouldn't live to see it. He died at Jezreel, on Mount Gilboa, in the battle where Saul also died, alongside his brothers Abinadab and Malchishua. The Philistines stripped his armor and his father's, mounted the bodies on the wall at Beth-shan. The men of Jabesh-gilead traveled through the night to take them down.

When David learns of the deaths, he tears his clothes and refuses to eat and writes a lament. The lament is in 2 Samuel 1. The line about Jonathan reads: "I am distressed for you, my brother Jonathan; very pleasant have you been to me; your love to me was extraordinary, surpassing the love of women." The text does not soften this. It records it. Translators and interpreters have debated it for centuries, and the text remains what it is.

The throne went to David. The name that survived belongs to David. Jonathan — the one who gave away his armor, who argued for David to the man who wanted him dead, who said you will be king and I will be second, as though that were fine — gets a footnote in the story he helped make possible.
