# Episode 34 — Nicodemus
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #5 (Question→A→C), Slot A #9 / Slot C #4
**Thesis:** Nicodemus appears three times in John's Gospel — once at night, once in a council meeting, once at a tomb — and the progression from secrecy to public action is the entire arc, told in three scenes across roughly three years.
**Grounded in:** John 3:1–21, John 7:50–52, John 19:38–42
**Tags used:** [whispering]

---

Why does a man come at night? The text is specific about the timing: Nicodemus came to Jesus by night. John's Gospel, which uses light and darkness as theological categories throughout, places this conversation in the dark. Was it fear? Was it caution? Was it simply the practical reality that a member of the ruling council couldn't afford to be seen consulting an unauthorized teacher from Galilee? The text doesn't say why. It says when. And then it records a conversation about being born again — which confused a man who understood everything about his tradition except, apparently, this.

Three appearances. Three moments. A man moving slowly toward something, and the text patient enough to show the pace.

I'm Naomi. Nicodemus.

[narration]

John 3 identifies him precisely. A Pharisee. A ruler of the Jews — meaning a member of the Sanhedrin, the seventy-one-member council that held religious and civil authority in Jerusalem under Roman oversight. He is not a peripheral figure. He has institutional power. And he opens the conversation with a concession: "Rabbi, we know that you are a teacher come from God, for no one can do these signs that you do unless God is with him." The plural "we" is notable — he may be speaking for a faction within the council, or he may be hedging his exposure. Either way, it is not a hostile opening. It is a man extending intellectual respect while staying carefully within the boundaries of what can be said.

[whispering] Jesus does not accept the premise of a collegial exchange. He responds with something that reframes the entire conversation: "Unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." The Greek word anothen means both "again" and "from above" — a double meaning the Gospel may be using deliberately. Nicodemus takes it literally: "How can a man be born when he is old? Can he enter a second time into his mother's womb?" Jesus presses further — born of water and Spirit, flesh and Spirit, the wind blowing where it wishes. Nicodemus says: "How can these things be?" Jesus responds: "Are you the teacher of Israel and yet you do not understand these things?"

That's the last thing Nicodemus says in chapter 3. The conversation continues — containing some of the most quoted verses in the New Testament — but Nicodemus disappears from it. We don't see him respond. We don't see him leave. He is simply no longer speaking.

Second appearance: John 7. It is the Feast of Tabernacles. The chief priests and Pharisees have sent officers to arrest Jesus, and the officers come back empty-handed, saying "No one ever spoke like this man." The Pharisees are furious. And then Nicodemus speaks up from within the council: "Does our law judge a man without first giving him a hearing and learning what he does?" It is not a declaration of faith. It is a procedural objection — a man using institutional language to create space for due process. The council turns on him: "Are you from Galilee too? Search and see that no prophet arises from Galilee." He says nothing more. But he said something. In public. In the council. That is movement.

Third appearance: John 19. Jesus is dead on the cross. Joseph of Arimathea — described as a secret disciple — asks Pilate for the body. And Nicodemus comes with him. He brings a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about seventy-five pounds. The quantity is enormous — a royal burial amount, extravagant in a way the text simply records. They wrap the body in linen with the spices, according to Jewish burial custom, and place it in a new tomb in a garden near the crucifixion site.

No words from Nicodemus in this scene. Just action. Just presence, in daylight, handling a body that would make him ceremonially unclean, in full public view, after the man he visited in secret has been executed by the state whose council he sat on.

The text gives you three data points and lets you draw the line yourself. Night. A procedural question. Seventy-five pounds of burial spices in broad daylight. That's the arc. That's Nicodemus. I'll see you in the next one.
