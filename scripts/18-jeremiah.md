# Episode 18 — Jeremiah
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #3 (A→B→C), Slot A #3 / Slot B #2 / Slot C #6
**Thesis:** Jeremiah is the prophet who objected to the job before it started, was ignored for the length of his entire career, bought real estate in a city he knew would be destroyed, and then had to live in the rubble long enough to write about it.
**Grounded in:** Jeremiah 1–52, Lamentations
**Tags used:** [dramatic]

---

Imagine being told, before you are old enough to have done anything significant with your life, that you were set apart for a specific purpose before you were born. Not a general purpose — a specific one. You will go where I send you and say what I tell you. And you say, with what sounds like genuine desperation: I don't know how to speak. I'm only a child. And the answer you get back is: don't say that. Don't say you're a child. You're going. Touch your mouth — now you have the words. Here is your assignment: tear down, overthrow, destroy, build, plant. Go.

And then you go. And the city you're sent to doesn't listen. And you spend your entire adult life saying things that won't change anything, to people who have already decided. And you know — because you've been told — that the walls are coming down regardless.

I'm Naomi Kessler. This is where we dig past the version of history everyone already agrees on. Today's name is Jeremiah.

[narration]

Jeremiah's call is dated to the thirteenth year of Josiah's reign, which puts it somewhere around 627 BCE. He will prophesy through the reigns of Josiah, Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin, and Zedekiah — across roughly four decades — until Jerusalem falls to Babylon in 586 BCE. That is a very long time to be saying things that are not welcome.

The early part of his ministry overlapped with Josiah's reform — a genuine religious restructuring of Judah, the rediscovery of the law scroll in the temple, the dismantling of foreign altars. Jeremiah is sometimes associated with supporting this reform, though the text doesn't make that completely explicit. What is explicit is that after Josiah died at Megiddo — killed in a battle he apparently had no business entering — things deteriorated quickly, and Jeremiah kept preaching to a city that was less and less interested.

[dramatic] The temple sermon in Jeremiah 7 is representative of the whole career. He stands at the gate of the house and tells the people not to trust in the phrase "the temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord" — as if the building itself were protection. He tells them to actually amend their ways. He tells them what happened to Shiloh, the earlier sanctuary that the Lord had already destroyed. A priest named Pashhur has him beaten and put in stocks for a night at the upper Benjamin Gate. When Jeremiah gets out, he gives Pashhur a new name — "terror on every side" — and tells him he'll die in Babylon.

At various points he is put in a muddy cistern and left there. He is imprisoned. He is thrown into another cistern so deep that he sinks in the mud and has to be pulled out with rags and old clothes tied to ropes. An Ethiopian court official named Ebed-melech arranges the rescue. That detail is in the text — the man who pulled Jeremiah out of the mud gets named, gets a prophecy of his own, gets told his life will be spared when the city falls.

The field purchase in Jeremiah 32 is one of the stranger acts in the Hebrew prophets. Jerusalem is actively under Babylonian siege. Jeremiah is in the courtyard of the guard. His cousin Hanamel shows up and offers to sell him a field in Anathoth. And Jeremiah buys it — weighs out the silver, signs the deed, seals it in a clay jar to preserve it, witnesses it properly. Then he prays and explicitly acknowledges: you told me to buy this, and also the city is going to fall. The act is described as a sign of hope — houses and fields will be bought again, eventually, after the exile. The purchase happens anyway. The city falls anyway.

Lamentations may or may not be his work — tradition attributes it to Jeremiah, modern scholarship is divided. Either way, someone sat in the ruins of Jerusalem and wrote it. Chapter 3 begins: I am the man who has seen affliction.

The people who didn't believe him believed him, eventually, when there was nothing left to change.

The thing the book of Jeremiah keeps returning to is a man who didn't ask for the job, couldn't get out of it, and couldn't make it work the way he wanted it to. Not failure — he said what he was told to say, accurately, for forty years. But not the kind of success that looks like success while it's happening. He wanted the words to do something, and mostly they didn't, and he kept giving them anyway. If you want more of this — the people who stayed in the work regardless of the result — that's what this channel is for. I'll see you in the next one.
