# Episode 40 — Mary Magdalene
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #9 (B(long)→A→C), Slot B #12 / Slot A #8 / Slot C #5
**Thesis:** Mary Magdalene is described in the text as a woman from whom seven demons had been cast out, who followed Jesus, who was present at the crucifixion and the burial, and who was the first witness to the resurrection — and was not believed. What centuries of tradition added on top of that is not in the text and is worth separating from it.
**Grounded in:** Luke 8:2, Matthew 27–28, Mark 15–16, Luke 24, John 20
**Tags used:** [surprised]

---

I don't do the highlight reel. I do the person. And Mary Magdalene is one of those names where the highlight reel has accumulated so many layers — centuries of tradition, artistic interpretation, theological debate, popular retelling — that finding the actual person underneath requires pulling quite a lot away. Most of what people carry as knowledge about her is not in the text. What the text actually records is stranger and more specific and, in some ways, more striking than the legend. So that is where we are going today.

Naomi here. Mary Magdalene is who we're doing today.

She is named for the town she came from — Magdala, on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. That is the origin of her name. Luke 8:2 introduces her as one of the women who accompanied Jesus and supported his ministry, and notes that seven demons had been cast out of her. That sentence is the entirety of what the text says about her background. It does not say when this happened, how it happened, or what the seven demons caused. It records that they were cast out and moves on. She is listed alongside other women who had been healed of various conditions, and she is listed first.

[surprised]

The tradition that identified Mary Magdalene as the sinful woman of Luke 7 — the unnamed woman who anoints Jesus's feet, who is described as having lived a sinful life — was made formally by Pope Gregory I in a sermon in 591 CE. It is not in the text. The text does not name that woman. The Eastern church never accepted the identification. It was quietly walked back by the Roman Catholic Church in 1969 when she was given her own separate feast day. The identification of her as a prostitute comes from the same conflation and shares the same problem: it is not in the text. The seven demons in Luke 8 are not explained as a reference to sexual sin or any other particular sin. They are simply recorded.

What the text does record, in some form in all four Gospels, is her presence at the crucifixion. Matthew and Mark say she watched from a distance along with other women. She stays. She is also present, in Matthew and Mark, when the body is placed in the tomb. She sees where it is laid.

On the first day of the week, she comes to the tomb early. All four Gospels record this, with differences in detail. In John 20, she comes alone, finds the stone has been removed, and runs to tell Peter and the beloved disciple. They come and see the empty tomb and leave. She stays. She stands outside weeping. She looks into the tomb and sees two angels. She turns, and there is a figure she does not recognize — she assumes he is the gardener, she asks where the body has been taken. He says her name.

That is the recognition scene. One word. She turns fully and says "Rabboni" — Teacher.

She is told: do not cling to me. Go and tell my brothers. She goes. John 20:18 records that she went to the disciples and said: I have seen the Lord. Mark 16:10-11 says she told those who had been with Jesus, who were mourning and weeping, and that they did not believe her. Luke 24:11 says the report from the women at the tomb seemed like nonsense to the disciples, and they did not believe them.

She was the first witness. She was sent to tell. She told. She was not believed.

The text does not say why she was chosen to be there, or why the announcement was given to her, or what happened to her after that morning. She moves through the Gospels primarily through these final scenes — the cross, the tomb, the recognition, the report. The tradition tried to fill in the before and the after. Most of what it filled in, it invented.

What is there, in the text, is enough. A woman who stayed when others left. Who came back in the dark. Who heard her name spoken and turned. Who was told to go and tell, and went and told, and was not believed.

The text believed her enough to record it.
