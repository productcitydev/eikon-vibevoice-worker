# Episode 20 — Abraham
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #9 (B(long)→A→C), Slot B #7 / Slot A #5 / Slot C #11
**Thesis:** Abraham told the same lie about his wife twice, to two different kings, in two different countries, and walked away enriched both times — and the text records this about the man it also calls a friend of God.
**Grounded in:** Genesis 12–25
**Tags used:** [chuckle]

---

I do history the way it actually happened, not the way it gets simplified. Which means sometimes you end up sitting with a thing that doesn't resolve cleanly — a pattern in a person that the text itself refuses to explain away, that the tradition built around them largely avoids, that the simplified version of the story skips entirely because it doesn't fit the summary.

This is Naomi. Which brings us to Abraham.

[narration]

There is a famine in Canaan. Abraham — at this point still called Abram — takes his household down to Egypt. Before they cross into Egypt, he stops and turns to his wife Sarai and says: I know you are a beautiful woman. When the Egyptians see you, they will say "this is his wife" and kill me. Tell them you're my sister. So that it will go well with me and my life will be spared on your account.

This is Genesis 12. Abraham has been called, given the covenant, promised land and descendants and blessing. He is not a young man. He has followed the voice to Canaan. And his immediate response to a famine is to go to Egypt and instruct his wife to lie about who she is so that he won't be killed for her.

It works. The Egyptians do notice Sarai. She is taken into Pharaoh's household. Abraham is given sheep, oxen, donkeys, servants, and camels in her name — payment for a woman presented as unmarried. Then God strikes Pharaoh's household with plagues. Pharaoh figures out what has happened, summons Abraham, and says: "What is this you have done to me? Why did you not tell me she was your wife? Take her and go." And Abraham goes — with everything, including everything he was given.

[chuckle] That's the first time.

The second time is in Genesis 20. Abraham is now in the Negev, in the territory of a king named Abimelech. And he tells Abimelech that Sarah — she has been renamed by now — is his sister. Abimelech takes her. Then God appears to Abimelech in a dream and tells him he is as good as dead because the woman is another man's wife. Abimelech, who hasn't touched her, points out that he was told she was Abraham's sister. God agrees that Abimelech acted in good faith, tells him to return the woman and have Abraham pray for him, and Abimelech does.

Then Abimelech calls Abraham in and asks him directly: what were you thinking? And Abraham explains. He says he told himself that surely there is no fear of God in this place, and they will kill me because of my wife. And he adds — and this is the part that changes the category of the act — she really is my sister, my father's daughter, though not my mother's daughter. And she became my wife. The lie was technically a half-truth. Sarah was, apparently, his half-sister. He had built a standing deception around a partial fact.

Abimelech gives Abraham sheep and oxen and male and female servants. He gives Sarah a thousand pieces of silver. Abraham prays, and the women of Abimelech's household — who had been made barren during the episode — are healed. Abraham leaves, again, with more than he arrived with.

The same situation, two different kings, two different countries. The same constructed story. The same result: Sarah returned, Abraham enriched, God intervening on their behalf without once being recorded as reproving Abraham for it. The kings are the ones who look better in both accounts — Pharaoh expels him without violence, Abimelech lectures him on ethics and gives him gifts anyway.

What the text does not do is explain this away. It does not offer a framework for understanding it. It sits in Genesis alongside Genesis 15, where Abraham believes God, and it is counted to him as righteousness. Alongside chapter 22, where he is called to sacrifice Isaac and doesn't flinch until the last moment. Alongside the covenant, the friendship, the title — friend of God, father of nations, the one through whom all the families of the earth will be blessed.

The same text holds all of it. The tradition has generally emphasized the covenant and faith, and said less about Egypt and Gerar. The record says both are part of the same person. A man who believed, and also a man who lied about his wife twice, to two different kings, because he was afraid. People are usually more than one thing. The patriarchs, apparently, are no different.
