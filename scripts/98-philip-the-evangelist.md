# Episode 98 — Philip the Evangelist

**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler (reverent-conviction register — warmth and gravity, not hype-speed)
**Format:** character-centered, full five-act depth. Opens on the distinction most people miss — this is not Philip the apostle but Philip the deacon, one of the Seven chosen in Acts 6, whose brief appearances in the text reveal a man consistently sent to boundaries: Samaritans, a eunuch, a desert road, and a coastline.

There are two Philips in the New Testament. Most people conflate them. Philip the apostle is one of the Twelve, listed in every gospel. Philip the evangelist is someone else entirely — one of the Seven chosen in Acts 6 to serve tables, a man who never held apostolic office but whose brief appearances in Acts trace the gospel's movement across every boundary the early church was afraid to cross. He goes where the apostles do not. He is consistently sent to the margins.

This is Naomi. Today's name is Philip — the evangelist, not the apostle.

He first appears in Acts 6 among the seven men of good repute chosen to resolve a dispute about food distribution between Hebrew and Greek-speaking widows. The text names him second, after Stephen. He is described with the same qualities: full of the Spirit and of wisdom. Stephen becomes the first martyr. Philip becomes the first missionary outside Jerusalem.

Acts 8: after Stephen's death, persecution scatters the church. Everyone except the apostles leaves Jerusalem. Philip goes down to a city in Samaria. This is not a small step. Samaritans and Jews did not share worship, did not intermarry, carried centuries of mutual contempt. Philip proclaims the Christ to them. Signs accompany him — unclean spirits come out, the paralyzed and lame are healed. The city believes. Simon the magician believes. The entire episode precedes Peter and John's arrival to confirm what has already happened. Philip was there first.

Then the text does something strange. An angel speaks to Philip directly: rise and go toward the south, to the road that goes down from Jerusalem to Gaza. The text adds: this is a desert place. No crowd. No city. One road in empty country. Philip goes.

On that road he finds a chariot carrying an Ethiopian eunuch — a court official of the Candace, queen of the Ethiopians, who has been to Jerusalem to worship and is now reading Isaiah 53 aloud. The Spirit tells Philip: go over and join this chariot. Philip runs. He hears the man reading. He asks: do you understand what you are reading? The eunuch answers: how can I, unless someone guides me?

Philip climbs into the chariot and, beginning from that scripture, tells him the good news about Jesus. They come to water. The eunuch says: what prevents me from being baptized? The question carries weight the text does not explain — a eunuch was, under Deuteronomic law, excluded from the assembly of the LORD. What prevents me? Philip baptizes him without hesitation.

Then Philip is gone. The text says the Spirit of the Lord carried Philip away. The eunuch sees him no more and goes on his way rejoicing. Philip is found at Azotus — ancient Ashdod, on the Mediterranean coast, miles from where he was. He passes through, preaching in all the towns until he reaches Caesarea.

The supernatural transport is remarkable, but what precedes it is more so. Philip was directed by an angel to a desert road to meet one person. Not a crowd. Not a city. One man in a chariot, reading a text he could not understand. The entire episode suggests that the boundary-crossing work of the gospel is sometimes not a movement but a meeting — one person, one road, one question asked at the right moment.

Acts 21:8 — years later, Paul and his companions stay at Philip's house in Caesarea. The text identifies him: Philip the evangelist, who was one of the seven. He has four unmarried daughters who prophesy. That is all the text gives — a house in Caesarea, a title earned by practice, and daughters who carry the same Spirit. He has settled on the coast, the boundary between land and sea, between Jewish territory and the wider world.

Eusebius records traditions about Philip's later life in Hierapolis, though he may confuse the two Philips. What Acts preserves is enough: a man who served tables, crossed ethnic lines, baptized the excluded, was supernaturally moved, and ended up on the coast where every boundary meets.

If you came here for the people the tradition overlooks — the ones doing the work without the title — that's what this channel does. See you next time.
