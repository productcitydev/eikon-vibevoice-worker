# Episode 36 — Isaiah
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #6 (Contrast→A→C), Slot A #11 / Slot C #10
**Thesis:** Isaiah saw the throne room, volunteered for the assignment, and was told immediately that the people would not listen — and he accepted the commission anyway, speaking for roughly forty years to a nation the text says would not hear.
**Grounded in:** Isaiah 1–66, 2 Kings 19–20, 2 Chronicles 26:22
**Tags used:** [dramatic]

A man volunteers for a mission. He says "Here am I — send me" before he knows what the mission is. Then God tells him: go, speak to this people. Make their hearts dull. Make their ears heavy. Shut their eyes. Lest they see with their eyes, hear with their ears, understand with their hearts, and turn and be healed. The man asks: how long? The answer: until cities lie waste without inhabitant, and houses without people, and the land is a desolation.

He said yes before the terms were disclosed. The terms, once disclosed, were that he would speak to people who would not respond, for a duration that would outlast his patience, toward an outcome that looked like failure by every measurable standard.

He did it for forty years.

I'm Naomi. Isaiah.

[narration]

The call narrative is chapter 6. "In the year that King Uzziah died, I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up, and the train of his robe filled the temple." Seraphim above him, each with six wings — covering face, covering feet, flying — calling to one another: "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory." The foundations of the thresholds shake. The house fills with smoke. Isaiah's response is not worship. It's terror: "Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts!"

A seraph flies to him with a burning coal taken from the altar with tongs, touches it to his mouth, and says: your guilt is taken away, your sin atoned for. Then the voice of the Lord: "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" Isaiah answers. He answers before the job description is given. "Here am I. Send me."

[dramatic] What follows is a prophetic career spanning approximately 740 to 700 BCE — possibly longer, depending on how one dates the later oracles. Isaiah operates under four kings: Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah. The political landscape is the Assyrian expansion under Tiglath-Pileser III, Shalmaneser V, Sargon II, and Sennacherib — a sequence of imperial threats that form the backdrop to nearly every oracle in the first thirty-nine chapters. Isaiah is not speaking into abstraction. He is speaking into active geopolitical crisis.

Under Ahaz, when Syria and northern Israel threaten Judah in the Syro-Ephraimite War of 735 BCE, Isaiah meets the king at the end of the aqueduct of the upper pool. He tells him to ask for a sign — any sign, as deep as Sheol or as high as heaven. Ahaz refuses, cloaking his refusal in piety: "I will not put the LORD to the test." Isaiah responds with the sign anyway — the Immanuel prophecy of chapter 7. Whether Ahaz wanted assurance or not, it was given. The prophet spoke regardless of the reception.

Under Hezekiah, the relationship is warmer. When Sennacherib's army besieges Jerusalem in 701 BCE — an event corroborated by the Assyrian Sennacherib Prism, now in the British Museum, which records the siege from the Assyrian side — Hezekiah sends to Isaiah for a word. Isaiah responds: the king of Assyria will not enter this city, will not shoot an arrow here, will return by the way he came. That night, the text says, the angel of the LORD struck down a hundred and eighty-five thousand in the Assyrian camp. Sennacherib withdrew. The secular record confirms the withdrawal without confirming the cause — the Prism records that Hezekiah was shut up "like a bird in a cage" but does not record the city falling.

The scope of Isaiah's writing — if one treats the book as substantially from one prophetic tradition — ranges from immediate political counsel to cosmic eschatology. Chapter 1 indicts Judah's worship as empty ritual. Chapter 40 opens with "Comfort, comfort my people." Chapter 53 describes a suffering servant who bears the iniquities of others. Chapter 65 envisions new heavens and a new earth. The range is extraordinary — from courtroom to throne room to a future the prophet himself would not live to see.

Tradition — not the biblical text but later Jewish sources, possibly reflected in Hebrews 11:37 — holds that Isaiah was martyred under King Manasseh, Hezekiah's son, by being sawn in two inside a hollow tree. The Talmud and the pseudepigraphal Ascension of Isaiah preserve this tradition. If true, the man who volunteered before knowing the terms ended his life under the worst king Judah would produce, killed for speaking what Manasseh did not want to hear.

He said send me. The terms were clear. He went anyway. I'll see you in the next one.
