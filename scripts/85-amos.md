# Episode 85 — Amos
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Format:** Character-centered, five-act depth; opens with a question about credentials before naming the subject
**Hook variant:** skeleton #5 (Question → A → C), Slot A #8 / Slot C #2
**Thesis:** Amos was not a prophet by profession or training — he was a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore figs — and his book is the earliest written prophecy in the Hebrew Bible, delivering an economic indictment so specific that it names the exact luxuries of the Israelite upper class.
**Epistemic honesty:** The dating of Amos to the mid-eighth century BCE is broadly accepted; the Samaria ivories discovered archaeologically corroborate the luxury culture he describes; some scholars consider the doxologies and the epilogue (9:11–15) to be later additions.

What qualifies a person to speak? A title, a lineage, a school? What happens when someone with none of those credentials stands in the center of a prosperous society and says: this is going to end, and here is why — and the why is not theological abstraction but a list of specific economic crimes? What happens when that person turns out to be right, and the record of what he said becomes the oldest written prophetic book we have?

Naomi here. Today, we're looking at Amos.

Amos 1:1 dates his words to the reigns of Uzziah king of Judah and Jeroboam II king of Israel — the mid-eighth century BCE. This is a period of prosperity. Israel's borders have expanded. Trade is flowing. The archaeological record from Samaria includes carved ivory furniture pieces from this period — luxury goods that match precisely what Amos describes and condemns.

Amos 7:14. When challenged by Amaziah, the priest of Bethel, Amos responds: I was neither a prophet nor the son of a prophet, but I was a shepherd, and I also took care of sycamore-fig trees. The LORD took me from tending the flock and said to me: go, prophesy to my people Israel.

He has no credentials. He is from Tekoa, in Judah — a small town in the wilderness south of Bethlehem. He is not from the northern kingdom he is addressing. He is not part of the prophetic guilds. He tends animals and cultivates figs. And he walks north to deliver an indictment.

[dramatic]

The structure of his book is precise. Chapters 1 and 2: a series of judgments against the nations surrounding Israel — Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab. Each follows the same formula: for three transgressions and for four, I will not turn back my wrath. The audience would have approved. Their enemies are being condemned. Then the formula turns — on Judah, and then on Israel itself. The trap is rhetorical. The listeners are agreeing with the standard of justice before it is applied to them.

The charges against Israel are not vague. Amos 2:6–8: they sell the innocent for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals. They trample on the heads of the poor as on the dust of the ground. Father and son use the same girl. They lie down beside every altar on garments taken in pledge. Amos 3:15: I will tear down the winter house along with the summer house; the houses adorned with ivory will be destroyed. Amos 4:1: you cows of Bashan on Mount Samaria, you women who oppress the poor and crush the needy and say to your husbands, bring us some drinks.

The specificity is what distinguishes Amos. He names the goods. He describes the houses. He identifies the judicial corruption — those who turn justice into bitterness and cast righteousness to the ground. He is not speaking in abstractions. He is describing an economy built on exploitation of the poor by the wealthy, and he is saying it will not survive.

Amos 5:21–24 contains what may be the most quoted passage in prophetic literature: I hate, I despise your religious festivals; your assemblies are a stench to me. But let justice roll on like a river, righteousness like a never-failing stream. The religion is not the solution. The religion, as practiced alongside the exploitation, is part of the offense.

Within decades of Amos's prophecy, the northern kingdom of Israel fell to Assyria in 722 BCE. The luxury culture he described was dismantled. The ivory houses were destroyed. The archaeological layer confirms the destruction.

Amos was not a prophet by training. He did not come from the system he addressed. He spoke to a society at the height of its prosperity and told it the prosperity was built on something that would not hold — and the record shows he was correct, within a generation.

That kind of voice — uncredentialed, specific, and vindicated — is rare in any tradition. If you have been here before, you know we take that seriously. If you have not — welcome. This is what we do.
