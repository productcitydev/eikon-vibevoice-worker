# Episode 08 — John the Baptist

**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #3 (A→B→C), Slot A #3 / Slot B #5 / Slot C #2
**Thesis:** John the Baptist is usually presented as the man who was certain — the voice in the wilderness who had no doubt. The record also preserves the question he sent from prison, and what Jesus said about him after the messengers left.
**Grounded in:** Luke 1; Matthew 3, 11, 14; Mark 6; John 1, 3
**Tags used:** [sigh]

---

[narration]

A man is sitting in a prison cell. He has spent his entire adult life — possibly his entire life, if the accounts of his calling are taken seriously — pointing at one specific person and saying: that's him. That's the one everyone's been waiting for.

From that cell, he sends messengers with a question.

"Are you the one who is to come, or should we expect someone else?"

I'm Naomi Kessler. Here, we don't do the polished version. We do the real one. Today, we're looking at John the Baptist.

Here's who he was before that question. According to Luke 1, John's birth was announced by an angel to a priest named Zechariah — his mother Elizabeth was elderly and hadn't been able to conceive. The angel was specific: this son would go before the Lord in the spirit and power of Elijah, to prepare the way. He was set apart before he was born. The text says he was filled with the Holy Spirit in the womb — which is the detail Luke uses to explain why, when the pregnant Mary visits Elizabeth, John leaps.

He grows up and goes into the wilderness. Locusts and wild honey. Camel hair. He doesn't fit into any institution. People come out to him — from Jerusalem, from all of Judea, the whole region of the Jordan — and he baptizes them, preaching repentance. The Pharisees and Sadducees show up and he calls them a brood of vipers to their faces.

When Jesus comes to be baptized, John says he's the one who should be baptized by Jesus, not the reverse. He does it anyway. John 1 records him telling his disciples directly: I am not the Christ. I'm not Elijah. I'm a voice. And when he sees Jesus walking by, he says — twice, on different days — behold, the Lamb of God.

He understood his role was to point, not to be the point.

But then Herod Antipas arrests him. John had publicly said Herod's marriage was unlawful — Herod had divorced his first wife to marry Herodias, his brother Philip's wife. That's what gets him locked up. And it's from that cell that the question is sent, recorded in both Matthew 11 and Luke 7.

"Are you the one, or should we expect someone else?"

The accounts don't explain what John was feeling. They don't interpret his inner state for us. But the question is allowed to stand, plainly, without apology. The man who announced Jesus to the world, who leaped before he was born, who said he wasn't even worthy to untie his sandals — from prison, he asks if he got it right.

Jesus's answer is careful. He doesn't say yes. He sends the messengers back with a list: the blind see, the lame walk, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, the poor hear good news. Then he adds one more line — one that seems addressed to John specifically, even though John isn't there: blessed is the one who does not stumble on account of me.

Then, after the messengers leave, Jesus turns to the crowd. What did you go out to see? A reed shaken by the wind? A man dressed in fine clothes? He says: I tell you, among those born of women, no one is greater than John.

That is the last public thing Jesus says about John while John is still alive.

The rest is quick. Herodias, who wanted John dead, used her daughter's dancing and Herod's rash oath to get the order. John was beheaded in prison. His disciples came and buried him, and went and told Jesus.

The text says Jesus withdrew to a solitary place.

[sigh]

What John got right was the assignment: prepare the way, point at the right person, decrease so someone else could increase. What the record also preserves — without softening it — is that the man spent his last weeks in a prison cell not entirely certain whether everything he'd given his life to was real.

Jesus's response to that doubt wasn't a proof. It was a list of things happening, and an invitation not to stumble.

Not the triumphant herald with no fear. A man who was great enough to have doubt, and who got an answer that asked him to trust what he could see. That's the full picture. That's who he was.
