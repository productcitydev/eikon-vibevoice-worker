Here is what we know from outside the texts that worship him. A Roman senator named Tacitus, writing around 116 CE, mentions that a man called Christus was executed under Pontius Pilate during the reign of Tiberius — and that the movement he started, far from dying with him, had spread to Rome and become a problem. Tacitus was not a sympathizer. He called it a "destructive superstition." But he recorded it as fact.

Josephus, the Jewish historian, mentions him twice. Pliny the Younger wrote to Emperor Trajan asking what to do about people who sang hymns to him "as to a god." Within eighty years of his death, the Roman bureaucracy was writing policy memos about him.

I go by Naomi here. Every other episode on this channel exists in the shadow of this one. This episode belongs to Jesus of Nazareth.

Here is the disproportion that demands attention: he was born in a backwater province, raised in a village so insignificant that a contemporary joke survives in the text — "Can anything good come from Nazareth?" He worked as a craftsman. He never held office. Never commanded an army. Never wrote a book. His public career lasted, by the most generous estimate, three years. He was executed by the state at approximately thirty-three, by a method reserved for slaves and political criminals.

And within three centuries, the empire that killed him worshipped him.

That fact alone — setting aside every theological claim — is historically unprecedented. No other figure of comparable obscurity has produced comparable consequences. Something happened. The minimum historical claim, accepted by virtually all scholars regardless of religious commitment, is this: Jesus existed, he was crucified under Pilate, and his followers sincerely believed something had happened after his death that convinced them he was alive. What that something was — that's where history ends and faith begins. But the sincerity of the belief is not in dispute. People don't get themselves killed for a story they know they invented.

The texts that tell his life say this: he taught with an authority that unsettled the religious establishment. He claimed a relationship with God that was either true or blasphemous — there was no safe middle reading. He healed. He forgave sins, which only God could do. He ate with people the system had written off. He made enemies not by breaking the law but by claiming to be above it.

The trial, when it came, turned on a single question: *Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?* His answer, according to Mark's account, was unambiguous: *I am.* That was enough. They handed him to Rome on a political charge — that he claimed to be a king — because theological blasphemy wouldn't interest a Roman prefect, but sedition would.

He died on a Friday afternoon. The texts record that his followers scattered. The movement should have ended there. A crucified messiah was, in the Jewish framework, a contradiction in terms — the messiah was supposed to win.

Then something happened on the third day.

The texts say resurrection. The skeptics have said hallucination, theft, swoon, legend. But here is the fact that none of those alternatives has adequately explained: the people who claimed to see him alive afterward gained nothing by it except persecution and death. James, his own brother — who did not follow him during his lifetime — became a leader of the movement and was executed for it. The James Ossuary, if authentic, bears his name: *James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus.* Josephus records his execution in 62 CE.

Something turned a group of terrified deserters into people willing to die for a claim they could have simply retracted. That something is the hinge of the entire story.
