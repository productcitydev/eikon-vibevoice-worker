Here's the contradiction. A young man stands at the edge of a crowd, watching another man get stoned to death. He doesn't throw a stone himself. He holds the coats of those who do — which, in context, means he approves. He has the authority to approve. He's educated, credentialed, zealous. He believes he is protecting God's honour by helping eliminate this new sect. The text names him: Saul of Tarsus.

That same man will write nearly half the books in the New Testament. Not after a gradual change of mind. After a single event on a single road.

I go by Naomi. On this channel, we look at who people actually were — the full, unedited version. This one belongs to Paul.

His credentials were not invented after the fact. He lists them himself, in his own letter to the Philippians: circumcised on the eighth day, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews, a Pharisee, trained under Gamaliel — one of the most respected teachers of the era. As to zeal, he says it plainly: a persecutor of the church. As to the law, blameless. He was not failing at his religion. He was excelling at it. That's what makes his reversal significant. This was not a man looking for an exit. This was a man at the top of his system who concluded the system itself was measuring the wrong thing.

The Damascus road event is described three times in Acts and referenced in his own letters. Whether one reads it as literal or visionary, what is historically undeniable is the outcome: a man with documents authorizing him to arrest followers of Jesus arrived in Damascus and within days was proclaiming Jesus in the synagogues. The people who knew him were terrified. The believers didn't trust him. He spent years in Arabia and Syria before anyone in Jerusalem would vouch for him. His transformation was not socially convenient. It cost him everything he'd built.

What followed was not a comfortable ministry. In Second Corinthians, he catalogues it himself: beaten with rods three times, received thirty-nine lashes five times, shipwrecked three times, spent a night and a day adrift at sea, in danger from rivers, bandits, his own people, Gentiles, in the city, in the wilderness, from false brothers. He is not boasting. He is making a point: if this were about comfort, credentials, or reputation, he would have stayed exactly where he was.

His letters — seven of which are considered authentic by virtually every critical scholar regardless of theological position — are not devotional pamphlets. They are arguments. Dense, structured, sometimes contradictory, always urgent. He dictated most of them. He was building theology in real time, under pressure, for communities that were falling apart in specific ways that required specific answers. Romans is a legal argument. First Corinthians is crisis management. Philemon is a personal plea about a runaway slave. These are not the writings of a mystic detached from the world. They are the writings of a man trying to hold something together while his body was being taken apart.

[sigh]

He died in Rome. Tradition says beheading — a Roman citizen's death, not crucifixion. Clement of Rome, writing within thirty years of the event, references his martyrdom as known fact. He never saw the movement become what it became. He never saw a church building. He wrote from chains, to people he might never see again, and said things like: "For me, to live is Christ, and to die is gain." That is not a slogan. From a man in his situation, it is an accounting statement. He ran the numbers. The math checked out.

The man who held coats at a stoning became the man who wrote the most influential letters in Western religious history — not because he found something he'd been missing, but because he concluded that everything he already had was, in his own word, rubbish. He used a stronger word in Greek. He meant it.

If you want more of the people behind the theology — what it actually cost them — that's what we do here. I'll see you in the next one.
