# Episode 06 — Pharaoh of the Exodus

**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #10 (Stakes→A→C), Slot A #2 / Slot C #4 (adapted — the name is withheld because the text withholds it)
**Thesis:** The shifting account of who hardened Pharaoh's heart — sometimes him, sometimes God — makes him the most theologically loaded figure in the Torah and raises a question about divine sovereignty and human responsibility that the tradition has never cleanly resolved.
**Grounded in:** Exodus 5–14, Romans 9:14–20
**Tags used:** [gasp] — at the moment the sea closes; the scale of what just happened

---

At some point in the middle of the ten plagues, the text quietly changes who is responsible for Pharaoh's stubbornness.

In the first several plagues, Pharaoh hardens his own heart — or the text simply describes his heart as hard, with no agent stated. Starting with the sixth plague, the boils, God hardens it. Same man. Same refusal. Different account of who is doing what. That shift appears in one of the most widely read texts in the ancient world, in plain view, and it raises a question that theologians have been arguing about for two thousand years: what does it mean for a man's choices to be God's doing? Is he being punished for refusing to yield, or is he being prevented from yielding so that the punishment can continue? Is this a story about justice, or about something the text is trying to say about sovereignty that still makes readers deeply uncomfortable?

My name's Naomi. That question, today, has a face — and a name the text never actually gives us. This is the Pharaoh of the Exodus.

The first thing worth noting: scripture does not name him. The popular historical identification is Ramesses II, partly based on the city called Ramesses in Exodus 1, but that identification is debated and not settled. The text doesn't care about his name. It cares about what he does and what happens because of it.

What he does: refuse. Consistently, almost rhythmically. A plague arrives. He summons Moses and Aaron. He promises to release the people if the plague is stopped. Moses prays. The plague ends. Pharaoh changes his mind and won't let them go.

This pattern holds across ten plagues — water to blood, frogs, gnats, flies, livestock disease, boils, hail, locusts, three days of darkness, death of the firstborn. Each time the cycle tightens. He negotiates. He relents enough to get relief. Then he doesn't deliver. The word the text keeps using, across different Hebrew roots, is the same in meaning: hard. His heart is hard. It goes hard. God makes it hard.

The progression of that hardening is worth following carefully. Exodus 4:21 tells us before any plague has happened that God will harden Pharaoh's heart. The plagues begin, and for the first five, Pharaoh hardens his own heart, or the text gives no agent. With the sixth plague, boils, God hardens it directly for the first time — and from there through the end, the divine causation is explicit. The text is not hiding the sequence. It's presenting it in order.

Paul wrestles with this in Romans 9. He quotes Exodus directly — "For this very purpose I have raised you up, to show my power in you, and so that my name may be proclaimed in all the earth." He takes it as evidence that God's purposes do not run on human willingness: mercy is God's to give; hardening is God's to do. He anticipates the objection immediately — "Why then does God still find fault? For who can resist his will?" — and his answer is less a resolution than a redirect: who are you, a human being, to answer back to God?

The text itself doesn't explain the mechanism. What it suggests through the sequence is something like this: Pharaoh's early refusals were his own. What God does later may be better read as fixing into place a pattern that already existed — not overriding a willing man, but using an already-unwilling one toward an end that will be visible to generations.

That reading doesn't resolve the question. It just shows you where the text leaves it.

By the tenth plague, every firstborn in Egypt has died — including Pharaoh's own son, though the text mentions this in passing without dwelling on it. Pharaoh summons Moses and Aaron in the middle of the night. He tells them to go. Take everything. Take your flocks. Go. Bless me also.

The people leave. And then Pharaoh changes his mind one more time and sends his army after them.

[gasp]

The sea closes. The army is gone. The man the text spent fourteen chapters building is not heard from again. No grief on the page. No reckoning. The narrative moves on.

What remains is the theological problem the text opened and didn't close. A man who refused, whose refusal God said he would harden, facing ten escalating consequences that God said he was using to make his name known — and the question of what culpability looks like inside all of that is still genuinely open.

He may be the most famous villain in the Old Testament. He might also be its most unresolved character.
