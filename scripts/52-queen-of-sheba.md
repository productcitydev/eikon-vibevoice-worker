# Episode 52 — Queen of Sheba

**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler (reverent-conviction register — warmth and gravity, not hype-speed)
**Format:** character-centered, full five-act depth. Opens on the stakes — a queen whose name the text never gives us, whose kingdom archaeology is still locating — then follows the single recorded visit and its theological weight as a test that Solomon passes.
**Hook variant:** skeleton #10 (Stakes→A→C), Slot A #2 / Slot C #4
**Thesis:** The text gives her no name, records a single visit, and preserves one sentence of her verdict — "the half was not told me" — and yet she is the only foreign ruler in the Hebrew Bible who comes not to conquer or negotiate but to test whether wisdom itself is real.
**Epistemic honesty:** the episode names the Sabaean kingdom of southern Arabia as archaeologically attested (monumental inscriptions, trade routes confirmed), while noting that no specific inscription has been linked to this queen or this visit, and that the Ethiopian tradition (Kebra Nagast) represents a separate and later literary tradition, not a biblical source.

A woman crosses a desert with a caravan of camels carrying gold, spices, and precious stones — an enormous quantity, the text specifies — to ask hard questions of a foreign king. Not to form an alliance. Not to negotiate a treaty. Not to marry him. To test whether what she has heard about his wisdom is true. She arrives. She asks everything she has prepared. And the text says he answered all of it. Nothing was hidden from him that he could not explain.

That question, today, has a name — well, almost. The text never gives her one.

My name's Naomi. That question, today, has a title: the Queen of Sheba.

First Kings 10. She comes because she has heard of Solomon's fame "concerning the name of the LORD." That phrase matters. She is not merely testing his intelligence or his administrative skill. The fame she has heard of is connected to something theological. She comes to test it with hard questions — the Hebrew word is chidot, which means riddles or enigmas, difficult questions that require more than surface knowledge to answer.

The text does not record the questions. It does not record the answers. It records only the result: he answered everything. Nothing was too difficult. And when she had seen all his wisdom, the house he had built, the food on his table, the seating of his officials, the attendance of his servants, their clothing, his cupbearers, and the burnt offerings he made at the temple of the LORD — the text says there was no more breath in her.

[whispering]

That is the phrase. No more breath in her. Not "she was impressed." Not "she was satisfied." The text uses the language of being undone by what she witnessed. And then she speaks — the one direct quotation the text preserves from her: "The report I heard in my own country about your achievements and your wisdom is true. But I did not believe these things until I came and saw with my own eyes. Indeed, not even half was told me."

The half was not told me. That sentence has traveled through three thousand years of interpretation.

She gives Solomon gold — 120 talents, which in any ancient measurement system is a staggering quantity — along with spices in quantities the text says were never again equaled, and precious stones. Solomon gives her everything she desires. She returns to her own country.

That is the entire biblical account. One visit. One test. One verdict. One departure.

The location of Sheba has been debated, but the scholarly consensus points to the Sabaean kingdom in what is now Yemen. Archaeological evidence from Marib — monumental temple ruins, dam infrastructure, trade inscriptions — confirms that a powerful, wealthy kingdom existed in that region during the relevant period, and that it controlled the incense trade route running north through Arabia. A queen traveling from there with camels bearing spices is historically plausible within that trade network. No inscription has been found naming this specific queen or this specific visit. The text is the primary source.

The Ethiopian tradition, preserved in the Kebra Nagast — a fourteenth-century compilation of older material — identifies her as Queen Makeda and claims she bore Solomon a son, Menelik I, who founded the Solomonic dynasty of Ethiopia. That tradition is deeply significant to Ethiopian Christianity and identity. It is not, however, in the biblical text. What is in the text is a visit, a test, a verdict, and a departure. Nothing more.

Jesus references her in Matthew 12:42 and Luke 11:31 — "the Queen of the South will rise at the judgment with this generation and condemn it; for she came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon, and now something greater than Solomon is here." He uses her as an example of someone who traveled an extraordinary distance to seek truth — and who recognized it when she found it.

She has no name in the text. She has no recorded homeland beyond a direction. She came, she tested, she was undone by what she found, she said one sentence the record has never stopped carrying, and she left.

The half was not told me. The text seems to think that is enough.
