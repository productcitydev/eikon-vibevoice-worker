# Episode 38 — Judas Iscariot
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #3 (A→B→C), Slot A #3 / Slot B #5 / Slot C #6
**Thesis:** The text records that Judas regretted the betrayal and returned the money — and uses a Greek word for that regret that is distinct from the word used elsewhere for repentance. What the text actually says, and what it leaves unresolved, keeps the theological question about Judas deliberately open.
**Grounded in:** Matthew 26–27, Mark 14, Luke 22, John 12–13, 17, Acts 1
**Tags used:** [dramatic]

---

A man returned thirty pieces of silver. He walked into the temple, threw the coins onto the floor, and said he had sinned — that he had betrayed innocent blood. The men he confessed to said: that is your problem. See to it yourself. He left the temple. The text records that he died, though the two accounts of his death differ enough that scholars have spent centuries trying to reconcile them. The thirty pieces of silver could not be returned to the treasury — it was blood money — so the chief priests used it to buy a potter's field for burying strangers. That field, the text says, became known as the Field of Blood, and the name persisted.

I'm Naomi Kessler. Here, we don't do the polished version. We do the real one. Today's name is Judas Iscariot.

He was one of the twelve. The Gospels name him last in their lists of the apostles, always with a note identifying him as the one who betrayed Jesus. John 12:6 says he kept the money bag and used to take from it — that is John's editorializing, placed at the scene where Judas objects to expensive ointment being poured out, arguing it could have been sold and given to the poor. John tells us the objection was not sincere. But the objection itself — that the money should have gone to the poor — is not, on its face, an unreasonable thing to say. The text gives us both things at once.

The betrayal is recorded in all four Gospels. In Matthew, Mark, and Luke, Judas identifies Jesus to the guards in Gethsemane with a greeting and a kiss. In John, Judas is present but the kiss is not mentioned — John has Jesus step forward and identify himself. The mechanism differs. The result does not.

The return of the silver appears only in Matthew 27. Judas, seeing that Jesus had been condemned, felt regret. The Greek word is metamelomai. It is not the word used elsewhere in the New Testament for repentance, which is metanoia. Whether that distinction is theologically significant or simply a difference in vocabulary has been argued for a long time. The text does not clarify it. Judas says he has sinned. He throws the money into the sanctuary. He goes out and hangs himself.

Acts 1:18 gives a different account of his death. It says Judas acquired a field with the payment for what he did, and falling headlong, his body burst open and his intestines poured out. Matthew says the priests bought the field after he returned the money. Acts says Judas acquired it himself. Matthew says he hanged himself. Acts describes a fall. Early harmonizations suggested the rope broke, which would reconcile the physical details but not the ownership of the field. As the texts stand, they do not agree.

The theological framing the Gospels apply to Judas is also not uniform. Luke 22:3 says Satan entered Judas before the betrayal. John 13:27 says the same — Satan entered him at the supper, after Jesus handed him a piece of bread. John 6:70 has Jesus referring to one of the twelve as a devil. John 17:12 says none was lost except "the son of destruction," so that scripture would be fulfilled. Acts 1 describes his death in terms of an office that needed filling — "his place of leadership let another take." The texts layer predestination language over moral language without resolving the tension between them.

What the text records: Judas was chosen. He took money. He identified the man. He returned the money. He said he had sinned. He died. The field was called the Field of Blood. His position was filled.

What the text does not settle: whether his regret was repentance in the fullest sense, whether what he did was foreordained in a way that diminishes moral responsibility, and what, if anything, that means for how his story ends. The church has argued about those questions since the first century. The text argues about them too, in its way — and never resolves them.

That unresolved quality may be the point. The field has a name. The question does not have an answer the text is willing to give you. Those two things stand together, and the text seems comfortable leaving them there.
