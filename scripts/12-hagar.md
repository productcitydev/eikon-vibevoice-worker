# Episode 12 — Hagar

**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** Skeleton #1 (A→C), Slot A #3 / Slot C #2
**Thesis:** Hagar, a slave woman with no agency in her own story, received two direct divine encounters — more than most named figures in Genesis — and the text records both without softening what was done to her.
**Grounded in:** Genesis 16, Genesis 21
**Tags used:** [sigh]

---

[narration]

Picture a woman dying of thirst in a desert — twice. Same woman. Same desert. Each time, she's been sent there by the same household: the household of the man who would become the founding patriarch of three of the world's major religions. She's not his wife. She's not his daughter. She's a slave — Egyptian, introduced by name only when the narrative needs her body to solve a problem. And yet she receives two direct encounters with God in the wilderness, is given two promises about her son's future, and becomes the only figure in the entire biblical text to personally give God a new name. Not Moses. Not Abraham. Not Sarah. A slave woman, alone, at a desert spring.

I'm Naomi Kessler. Today, we're looking at Hagar.

She enters Genesis 16 with almost no introduction: an Egyptian woman, slave of Sarai, wife of Abram. We don't know how she came to be in that household — Egypt is a long way from Canaan, and the text doesn't explain the journey. We don't know her age, her family, her life before the record picks her up. What we know is that Sarai, unable to conceive, followed a custom well-documented in the ancient Near East: she gave her slave to Abram so that a child could be legally attributed to Sarai's household. This was not unusual in its cultural context. It was a known legal mechanism. Hagar did not choose it. The text doesn't record her opinion. It says Sarai gave her.

When Hagar became pregnant, the dynamic shifted. The text says she looked on Sarai with contempt — the only act of agency we see from her in this part of the story. Sarai complained to Abram. Abram said, essentially: she's your slave, do whatever seems right to you. Sarai dealt harshly with Hagar. And Hagar ran.

She fled to the wilderness, to a spring of water on the road to Shur. And this is where the first encounter happens. The angel of the LORD finds her and asks two questions: Where have you come from? Where are you going? She answers the first honestly: I'm running from my mistress. She doesn't answer the second. She doesn't have an answer. She's just running.

The angel tells her to return and submit. And then — unprompted, unsolicited — he gives her something she didn't ask for: a promise. A son, to be named Ishmael, which means "God hears." A lineage too numerous to count. The words given to Hagar echo the words given to Abraham himself. She receives them alone, at a desert spring, from a divine messenger no one else in the narrative sees.

Then Hagar does something extraordinary. She names God. She calls him El-roi — the God who sees me. And she says: "Have I really seen God and remained alive?" She is the only person in all of Scripture to give God a personal name. Not a patriarch. Not a prophet. A runaway slave woman, alone, who had an encounter she could only describe as: he saw me.

She goes back. She has the child. His name is Ishmael. Abram is eighty-six years old. They live, for a time, within the same household.

Years pass. Sarah — her name has changed — gives birth to Isaac. At the celebration of Isaac's weaning, Sarah sees Ishmael and makes a decision. The text's description of what Ishmael was doing has been translated differently across manuscript traditions — laughing, playing, mocking — but whatever Sarah saw, her verdict was immediate: send them away. He will not share the inheritance with Isaac.

Abraham was distressed. The text says so. God tells him to listen to Sarah, and promises that Ishmael will also become a great nation. Abraham rises early, gives Hagar bread and a skin of water, and sends her and the boy into the wilderness of Beersheba.

The water runs out. Hagar puts the child under a shrub because she cannot bear to watch him die. She walks away — the text specifies the distance: about a bowshot — and sits down, and weeps.

She does not pray. She does not petition God. She just weeps.

The angel of God calls out to her from heaven — not to the boy, to her. "What troubles you, Hagar?" Not a rebuke. A question. The angel tells her Ishmael will live, will become a great nation. And then God opens her eyes and she sees a well of water. She fills the skin. She gives the boy a drink.

[sigh]

Both encounters follow the same shape: Hagar is at her lowest point, with no recourse and no advocate, and God comes to her directly. Not through Abraham. Not through a priestly intermediary. Directly, and by name. The text records Sarah's harshness in the first story without editorial comment. It records Abraham's silence in the second. It doesn't soften what was done to her or explain it away. It just reports it — and then reports that God was there anyway.

Hagar is not a saint figure in the text. She's not given a theology or a calling beyond her son's survival. She's a woman who was owned, used, expelled twice, and who named God in the wilderness because there was nothing else left to name. And the text, without flinching, says he heard her.

That's the whole story. And it's more than most named figures in Genesis receive.
