# Episode 64 — Titus
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Format:** Character-centered, five-act depth; opens on the stakes of his absence from Acts
**Hook variant:** skeleton #10 (Stakes statement → A → C), Slot A #9 / Slot C #8
**Thesis:** Titus was the man Paul trusted with the hardest assignments — Corinth at its worst, Crete from scratch — and the fact that Luke never mentions him in Acts, despite his central role in Paul's letters, remains one of the genuinely unexplained silences in New Testament scholarship.
**Epistemic honesty:** His absence from Acts is noted by scholars but not definitively explained; the tradition of his bishopric in Crete comes from Eusebius and later sources, not from the canonical text.
**Paralinguistic tags:** none
**Opening hook:** ~115 words / ~10 seconds
**Length:** ~690 words / ~3,950 characters / ~4:45
**Grounded in:** Galatians 2:1–3, 2 Corinthians 2, 7–8, 12, Titus 1–3, 2 Timothy 4:10, Eusebius (Church History III.4.6)

---

There is a man who does not appear in Acts. Not once. The book that tracks Paul's journeys across thousands of miles, that names traveling companions, ship captains, hosts, and local officials — this book has no room for him. And yet, in Paul's own letters, he is one of the most trusted people in the operation. Paul calls him partner and fellow worker. Paul sends him into the messiest situation in the early church and trusts him to fix it. Paul leaves an entire island in his hands. The silence in Acts is not casual. It has generated theories for centuries. None of them are settled.

It's Naomi. Titus. That's today's subject.

He appears first in Galatians 2. Paul brings him to Jerusalem — a Greek, uncircumcised — as a test case. The question on the table: must Gentile converts be circumcised? Paul says Titus was not compelled to be circumcised, even though false brothers had infiltrated the group and were pushing for it. The fact that Titus remained uncircumcised became, in Paul's telling, evidence that the Jerusalem leaders affirmed the gospel as Paul preached it. Titus is not speaking in this scene. He is standing there, being the proof.

Then Corinth. Second Corinthians tells the story in fragments, across several chapters. Paul had sent Titus to Corinth during a period of serious tension between Paul and that community. There had been a painful visit. There had been a severe letter. Paul was anxious — he went to Troas hoping to find Titus there with news, and when Titus was not there, Paul could not rest. He moved on to Macedonia. And there, finally, Titus arrived with good news. The community had responded. They were grieved. They were repentant. Paul writes that God, who comforts the downcast, comforted him by the coming of Titus.

That sequence tells you what Titus could do. Paul did not send a letter and hope. He sent a person. Titus walked into a community that was hostile to Paul's authority, carried a message that could have made things worse, and brought them around. Second Corinthians 8 shows Paul sending him back again — this time to organize the collection for Jerusalem. Paul says Titus accepted the appeal and is coming to them of his own accord. He calls him earnest. He calls him his partner.

The epistle to Titus places him on Crete. Paul says he left him there to put in order what remained to be done and to appoint elders in every town. The letter gives instructions — qualifications for leadership, how to address different groups, how to handle divisive persons. The assumption is that Titus is capable of building structure from very little. The island has new believers and no established leadership. Titus is the one left behind to create it.

Second Timothy 4:10, near the end of Paul's life, says Titus has gone to Dalmatia. One sentence. No explanation. That is the last canonical mention.

Eusebius, writing in the early fourth century, records a tradition that Titus served as the first bishop of Crete. Whether this reflects historical memory or later construction is not certain. The tradition exists. It fits what the epistle describes. It cannot be independently verified.

The absence from Acts has never been resolved. Some scholars have suggested Titus was Luke's brother, which would explain why Luke — who avoids naming himself — might also avoid naming a close relative. Others have proposed different reasons. None are conclusive. The silence remains.

What we have, then: a Greek who stood as a test case in Jerusalem and was not forced to comply. A man trusted with Corinth at its most fractured. A builder left alone on an island to make order out of nothing. A name that Paul reaches for when the assignment is too sensitive to entrust to instruction alone.

He is never quoted. He never speaks in the text. What he did is recorded only in what Paul says about sending him and what Paul felt when he returned. The text holds him through someone else's relief.
