# Episode 91 — Ishmael

**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler (reverent-conviction register — warmth and gravity, not hype-speed)
**Format:** character-centered, full five-act depth. Opens on the fact that God names Ishmael before he is born — one of the few figures in Scripture given a divine name in utero — then traces the distance between that distinction and his expulsion into the wilderness.
**Hook variant:** skeleton #3 (A→C→B), Slot A #4 / Slot C #10 / Slot B #7
**Thesis:** Ishmael is remembered as the one who was sent away, but the text insists — repeatedly — that God heard him, named him for that hearing, and made him a nation; the rejection was Abraham's household arrangement, not God's final word.
**Epistemic honesty note:** the episode acknowledges that Ishmael's twelve sons listed in Genesis 25 correspond broadly to tribal groups attested in later Assyrian inscriptions (Nebaioth, Kedar), though precise identification remains debated among scholars.

Ishmael. The firstborn of Abraham. Named by God before he drew breath.

It's Naomi. This is what the record actually says about today's subject.

Most people encounter Ishmael as a theological footnote — the other son, the wrong line, the one who had to leave so the promised child could arrive. But the text introduces him differently. In Genesis 16, the angel of the LORD finds Hagar fleeing into the wilderness and says: you shall call his name Ishmael, because the LORD has heard your affliction. The name itself means "God hears." Before this child exists, he is named for divine attention. That is not how the text introduces someone it intends to discard.

Hagar returns. Ishmael is born. Abraham is eighty-six years old. For thirteen years, Ishmael is the only son. When God establishes the covenant of circumcision in Genesis 17, Abraham circumcises Ishmael alongside himself. Ishmael is thirteen — old enough to understand, old enough to participate. Abraham even pleads: oh that Ishmael might live before you. God's answer is precise: the covenant line will pass through Isaac, who is not yet born. But regarding Ishmael — I have heard you. There is the name again. I have blessed him. I will make him fruitful. Twelve princes shall he father, and I will make him a great nation.

Isaac is born. The household fractures. Genesis 21: Sarah sees Ishmael — the Hebrew word used is sometimes translated "mocking," sometimes "laughing," sometimes simply "playing" — and demands his expulsion. Abraham is grieved. God tells him to listen to Sarah, but adds: of the son of the slave woman I will also make a nation, because he is your offspring.

[dramatic]

The expulsion scene is stark. Abraham rises early, gives Hagar bread and a skin of water, puts the child on her shoulder, and sends them away. She wanders in the wilderness of Beersheba. The water runs out. She places the boy under a bush because she cannot bear to watch him die. She sits a bowshot away and weeps.

And God heard the voice of the boy. The angel of God calls from heaven: what troubles you, Hagar? Fear not, for God has heard the voice of the boy where he is. The name delivers on its own meaning. Ishmael — God hears. And God heard. A well appears. They survive.

The text says: God was with the boy, and he grew up. He lived in the wilderness of Paran. He became an expert with the bow. His mother found him a wife from Egypt. These are spare details, but they are present — the text tracks him.

Genesis 25 records that when Abraham dies, both Isaac and Ishmael bury him together in the cave of Machpelah. The estrangement of the household did not, apparently, survive Abraham's death. Both sons stand at the grave. The text names them side by side without comment on the reunion.

Then the genealogy: Ishmael's twelve sons, listed by name, described as twelve princes according to their tribes. Nebaioth, Kedar, Adbeel, Mibsam — names that appear centuries later in Assyrian records as tribal confederations of the Arabian peninsula. The promise of twelve princes was not metaphor. It became geography.

Ishmael dies at one hundred thirty-seven. The text records it plainly. He is not cursed. He is not condemned. He is a man who was named by God before birth, blessed in covenant, expelled by household politics, preserved in the wilderness by divine intervention, and made the father of nations. The text does not treat him as a villain. It treats him as someone whose story ran parallel to the covenant line rather than through it — which is a different thing entirely.

The tradition has often made Ishmael carry theological weight the text itself does not assign him. The text assigns him a name that means God hears, and then demonstrates — three separate times — that God did.

That distinction between what the text says and what the tradition assumes — that's the work we do here. See you next time.
