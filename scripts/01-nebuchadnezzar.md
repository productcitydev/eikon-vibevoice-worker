Picture the most powerful man on earth, on his hands and knees in a garden, eating grass.

Not a metaphor. Not a fever dream. According to the account, for a period measured in years, the king who had conquered the known world lived outside his own palace, hair grown wild, nails grown into claws, eating grass like the animals he probably once hunted for sport.

He built that palace himself. He tore down someone else's kingdom to get the materials.

This channel exists for exactly one thing: the people history either got wrong or skipped entirely — and somehow, despite being one of the most powerful men who ever lived, that includes him. I go by Naomi here. This episode belongs to Nebuchadnezzar.

Here's who he actually was before any of that happened. Nebuchadnezzar the Second ruled Babylon for over forty years — not a legendary figure dressed up for scripture, but a documented historical king. His own building inscriptions survive. In them, in his own words carved into brick, he boasts about the palaces and temples he built "by my mighty power" — almost the exact phrase that shows up in his story right before everything falls apart. We're not guessing at his personality. He told us who he thought he was, in stone, and it matches.

He conquered Jerusalem. Destroyed its temple. Took a generation of its brightest young men back to Babylon to be reshaped into loyal Babylonian officials — among them, four teenagers who refused to eat the king's food because it violated their law. That refusal is where Nebuchadnezzar first collides with the God he'd spend the rest of his life either defying or bowing to, depending on the day.

He wasn't stupid, and he wasn't uninterested. When one of those exiles — Daniel — correctly described and interpreted a dream Nebuchadnezzar hadn't told anyone, the king fell on his face and called Daniel's God "God of gods." Then he went right back to being exactly who he was.

Years later, three of those same exiles refused to bow to a ninety-foot gold statue Nebuchadnezzar had built of himself. He had them thrown into a furnace heated so hot it killed the men who threw them in. They walked out untouched. Nebuchadnezzar watched it happen, decreed that no one in his empire could speak against their God — and still didn't change.

Twice now, undeniable evidence. Twice, no lasting difference. That's the pattern worth sitting with: this wasn't a man who didn't know. He kept almost getting there and stopping short.

Then came a second dream — a massive tree, cut down to a stump at a voice's command, bound in iron and bronze. Daniel told him plainly what he probably already suspected: the tree was him, and the cutting-down was coming, unless he changed course. Humble yourself, Daniel said. Nebuchadnezzar didn't.

A year passed. Nothing happened. And then, walking on his palace roof, looking out over the city he'd spent his life building, he said the sentence that undid him: *Is this not great Babylon, which I have built by my mighty power, for the glory of my majesty?*

The words weren't even finished leaving his mouth. According to the account, he was driven from his throne that instant — his mind gone, living like the animals he'd have considered beneath him, for seven periods of time, until, the text says, he lifted his eyes toward heaven.

And here's the detail that makes this story unlike almost anything else in scripture: what happens next is written in his own voice. First person. The most powerful pagan king in the biblical record, in his own recorded words, saying his sanity returned the moment he stopped trying to explain his own greatness and started acknowledging something bigger than it. *I blessed the Most High,* he says. *His kingdom is an everlasting kingdom.*

[sigh]

That's the man on his knees in the garden. Not a fool, and not entirely a monster — a man who built an identity so completely out of his own power that the only thing strong enough to break it was losing his mind, and the only thing that could put it back together was admitting, in his own handwriting, that he hadn't been the biggest thing in the room the whole time.

If you want more of this — the names history left out, and the ones it got wrong — that's exactly what we're doing here. I'll see you in the next one.
