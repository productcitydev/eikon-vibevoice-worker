# Episode 10 — Josiah

**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #4 (A only, C folded in), Slot A #9
**Thesis:** Josiah found the lost Book of the Law, wept when he heard it, launched the most thorough religious reform in Judah's history, was promised he'd die before the coming disaster — and then chose a battle he'd been told to stay out of, and died in it anyway.
**Grounded in:** 2 Kings 22–23; 2 Chronicles 34–35; Jeremiah (lament reference)
**Tags used:** [sigh]

---

[narration]

They found a book in the Temple.

Not a new book — an old one, lost somewhere during who knows how many years of neglect, rediscovered by a priest during what was meant to be a routine renovation project. He brought it to the royal secretary. The secretary read it to the king.

The king tore his clothes.

It's Naomi. And the king who tore his clothes was Josiah — a man who did everything right, changed an entire nation, was promised he would be spared the coming disaster, and then rode into a battle he had been explicitly told to avoid.

Here's the context. Josiah became king of Judah when he was eight years old. His father Amon had been assassinated after two years on the throne. His grandfather Manasseh had ruled for fifty-five years and was, by the account in 2 Kings, the worst king Judah ever produced — he rebuilt the high places his father Hezekiah had demolished, placed altars to foreign gods inside the Temple itself, practiced divination, and, the text says, filled Jerusalem with innocent blood from one end to the other.

Josiah came out of that. The record in 2 Chronicles says that in his eighth year of reign — when he was sixteen — he began seeking the God of his ancestors. In his twelfth year, he started tearing down the high places, the Asherah poles, the carved idols, the altars to Baal. In his eighteenth year, while the Temple renovation was underway, the priest Hilkiah found the Book of the Law.

No one is entirely certain which text this was. Tradition usually identifies it with some portion of Deuteronomy, though scholars have debated the specifics for a long time. What the account makes clear is that the king heard it read and understood immediately: his people had not been keeping it. For a long time. Possibly for generations.

He sends a delegation to a prophetess named Huldah. Not to the prophet Jeremiah, who was already active at that time — Huldah. Her answer is specific: the judgment in the book is real and coming, because Judah has burned incense to other gods. But because Josiah humbled himself and wept when he heard the words, he personally will not see the disaster. He will be gathered to his grave in peace.

Take that and hold it for a moment. He is told: you did right, the judgment is still coming for the nation, and you are protected from it personally. That could have been an invitation to step back and let events unfold. It wasn't.

Josiah then launches the most sweeping religious reform in Judah's history. He reads the book publicly to every inhabitant of Jerusalem. He dismantles everything connected to foreign worship — the Asherah poles, the altars to Baal, the Topheth in the Valley of Hinnom where children had been burned as offerings. He goes north into the territory of the former kingdom of Israel and does the same there. 2 Kings 23 says plainly that no king before him had turned to God with all his heart and soul and might as Moses's law commanded — and none came after him.

He also keeps a Passover that 2 Chronicles says had no parallel since the time of the judges. The account is specific about the logistics: the priests in their divisions, the Levites in their assigned roles, the Passover lambs provided at royal expense.

Then, years later, Pharaoh Necho of Egypt marched north toward Carchemish — a conflict that had nothing to do with Judah. Josiah went out to stop him. Necho sent messengers saying: I have no quarrel with you, king of Judah. God has commanded me to hurry. Turn back. 2 Chronicles 35 records this detail carefully — Necho's message claimed to carry divine authority. And Josiah did not listen. He disguised himself and went into battle anyway. An arrow struck him. He was brought back to Jerusalem. He died there.

Jeremiah composed a lament for him. The mourning became formalized — 2 Chronicles says it was established as a custom in Israel. The nation's grief was specific and lasting.

[sigh]

There is no explanation in the text for why Josiah didn't listen. The prophecy said he'd die before the disaster, gathered in peace — and then he died in battle from an arrow wound. Commentators have worked at that tension for centuries, and I won't pretend it resolves cleanly, because the text doesn't resolve it.

What it leaves us with is a king who understood the law better than any before him, who tore down everything that needed tearing down, who was told he was protected — and who still rode into a fight he'd been warned away from, and didn't come back.

The reform stands. The Passover stands. The grief stands. The question at the end stands too.
