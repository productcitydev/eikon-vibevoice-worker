# Episode 41 — Barabbas
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #7 (A→C, no B), Slot A #11 / Slot C #9
**Thesis:** Barabbas's name in Aramaic means "son of the father." He was released in place of Jesus. The text gives him almost no individual detail and no account of what happened after he walked free — and that absence carries the entire theological weight of his story.
**Grounded in:** Matthew 27, Mark 15, Luke 23, John 18
**Tags used:** none

---

His name means son of the father. Bar — son. Abba — father. That is what his name means. He was in prison. The texts describe him as a notable prisoner, arrested for insurrection and murder — Mark and Luke say insurrection, John calls him a bandit, using a Greek word, lestes, that can mean rebel or robber depending on the context. Matthew just calls him notable. The custom described in all four Gospels is that at Passover, the governor released one prisoner, whoever the crowd requested. The crowd was asked. They chose him. Another man was sent to be crucified in his place. The texts record Barabbas's release and then move on. He has no further scenes. He does not appear again. We have no account of what he did afterward.

Naomi Kessler — this episode belongs to Barabbas.

Four texts. Almost no individual character. Matthew 27, Mark 15, Luke 23, John 18. All four record the exchange. None of them give him a face.

What we have: he was in prison. He was known — Matthew calls him notable, which implies he had a reputation. He had been involved in some kind of uprising, one that included killing, though we are not told the circumstances of either the insurrection or the death. He was one of the prisoners the crowd could have chosen. They chose him.

That is what we have.

The name is the thing that has drawn the most attention, and it is easy to see why. Bar-abba. Son of the father. The irony is not subtle — the man named "son of the father" is released so that the man who called himself the Son of the Father could be executed. The texts do not comment on this. They simply record the name and the exchange. Whether the naming is coincidence, or whether the story has been shaped around it, or whether it is the kind of coincidence that the tradition decided to carry forward because it was too significant to leave unmarked — none of that is answerable from the text. But the name is there.

There is a textual note worth knowing. Some early manuscripts of Matthew 27 give the prisoner's full name as Jesus Barabbas. The name Jesus was common in first-century Judea — it is the Greek form of Joshua. A small number of manuscripts preserve it; Origen, the third-century theologian, mentions the name but says it seemed wrong to him that a sinner should carry the name Jesus. Whether those manuscripts represent an original reading or an early scribal tradition is debated. Most modern translations do not include it, but it sits in the textual history.

If the full name is Jesus Barabbas, then Pilate asks: which Jesus do you want released? Jesus, son of the father — or Jesus, called the Christ? And the crowd chooses one.

After that, the texts have nothing. No record of where Barabbas went. No tradition with reliable grounding about what became of him. He does not appear in any subsequent account, any contemporaneous document, any credible later source. He walked out and the record ends.

That absence has produced a question that two thousand years of readers have not been able to stop asking: did he know? Did he ever hear what happened to the man released in his place? Did he understand what the exchange meant, or what it was being interpreted to mean by the people who were there? Did he seek out information about it, or spend the rest of his life avoiding the subject?

The text does not provide any of this. It cannot. Barabbas is not what the texts are following. They are following the other man. Barabbas is the door through which the story passes on its way to something else.

But he was real. There was a man, in a Roman prison in Jerusalem, who walked out because the crowd said his name. Whatever that morning felt like to him — relief, confusion, indifference, something he never found words for — is gone from the record.

What remains is the exchange, and the name, and the field of silence where his story should continue and does not.
