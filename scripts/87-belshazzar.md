# Episode 87 — Belshazzar
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Format:** Character-centered, five-act depth; opens with the dramatic image of the writing on the wall before pulling back to the historical question
**Hook variant:** skeleton #9 (B(long) → pause → A(short) → C(short)), Slot B #2 / Slot A #9 / Slot C #12
**Thesis:** Belshazzar was dismissed by scholars for over two thousand years as a biblical fiction — no king by that name appeared in any known record — until 1854, when cuneiform cylinders confirmed his existence as the son of Nabonidus and co-regent of Babylon, making him one of the most striking cases of the archaeological record catching up to the biblical text.
**Epistemic honesty:** The Nabonidus Cylinder and the Verse Account confirm Belshazzar's historicity and his co-regency; however, Daniel calls him "king" and "son of Nebuchadnezzar," which does not match the Babylonian records (Nabonidus was his father, not Nebuchadnezzar). The relationship to Nebuchadnezzar may be indirect or the term "son" used loosely.

This is where we dig past the version of history everyone already agrees on. And sometimes, the version everyone agreed on was that a person did not exist at all. For over two thousand years, no record outside the Bible mentioned a king of Babylon named Belshazzar. Scholars listed him as evidence of the book of Daniel's unreliability — a fictional character in a fictional court. There was no such king. Then, in 1854, Henry Rawlinson published cuneiform inscriptions from a clay cylinder found in Ur. The cylinder belonged to Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon. It names his firstborn son: Bel-shar-usur. Belshazzar. He was real.

It's Naomi. So — Belshazzar.

[gasp]

The Nabonidus Cylinder and the Verse Account of Nabonidus — both recovered from Mesopotamian archaeological sites — establish the following: Nabonidus was the last Neo-Babylonian king. He was absent from Babylon for extended periods, residing in the Arabian oasis of Tayma for approximately ten years. During his absence, he entrusted the kingship to his son Belshazzar. Belshazzar exercised royal authority in Babylon but was never formally the king — he was co-regent, acting in his father's stead.

This explains a detail in Daniel 5 that had puzzled commentators. When Belshazzar offers a reward to whoever can read the writing on the wall, he promises to make that person the third highest ruler in the kingdom. Not second. Third. Because Belshazzar himself is only second — Nabonidus holds the first position. The text preserves an administrative detail that makes sense only if the author understood the co-regency arrangement.

Daniel 5. Belshazzar holds a great feast for a thousand of his lords. He commands that the gold and silver vessels taken from the temple in Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar be brought out. They drink from them. They praise the gods of gold and silver, bronze, iron, wood, and stone.

Then — the fingers of a human hand appear and write on the plaster of the wall, near the lampstand. The king watches the hand as it writes. His face turns pale. His knees knock together. His legs give way. He calls for the enchanters, the astrologers, the diviners. None can read the writing.

The queen — likely the queen mother — enters and says: there is a man in your kingdom in whom is the spirit of the holy gods. In the days of your predecessor he was found to have insight and intelligence. Call for Daniel.

Daniel is brought in. He refuses the gifts. He reads the inscription: MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN. Numbered, numbered, weighed, divided. God has numbered the days of your kingdom and brought it to an end. You have been weighed on the scales and found wanting. Your kingdom is divided and given to the Medes and Persians.

That very night, the text says, Belshazzar king of the Chaldeans was slain. Darius the Mede received the kingdom.

The fall of Babylon to Cyrus is recorded by multiple sources. Herodotus describes the Persians diverting the Euphrates and entering under the walls. Xenophon mentions a feast happening inside the city on the night of its capture. The Nabonidus Chronicle — a Babylonian record — confirms that Babylon fell to Cyrus's forces in October 539 BCE with minimal resistance. Nabonidus was captured. The chronicle does not name Belshazzar's fate specifically, but it records that the city was taken during a festival.

The convergence is striking. A feast. A city taken at night. A co-regent exercising authority while the actual king is elsewhere. The cuneiform record and the biblical narrative align on the structure of the event, even where they differ on specific details.

Daniel calls Belshazzar the son of Nebuchadnezzar. The Babylonian records show Nabonidus was his father, not Nebuchadnezzar. The term "son" in Aramaic can mean descendant or successor — and Belshazzar's mother may have been a daughter of Nebuchadnezzar, which would make the connection familial if indirect. The relationship is debated. What is not debated any longer is that he existed.

A man dismissed as fiction for two millennia, confirmed by clay. That is what the record does sometimes — it waits.

If you are new here, this is what we do. One name, one record, every time.
