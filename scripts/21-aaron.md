# Episode 21 — Aaron
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #7 (A→C no B), Slot A #11 / Slot C #3
**Thesis:** Aaron built the golden calf and then claimed he hadn't, watched his sons die for a ritual error and was told not to grieve them publicly, and spent his career as the second voice in a story that required a first.
**Grounded in:** Exodus 4, 7–8, 32; Leviticus 10; Numbers 12, 20
**Tags used:** [groan]

A man is told to explain himself. His brother has come down from a mountain with two stone tablets and found that the community has built an idol from melted gold in his absence, and the man standing in front of him is the one responsible for it. And the explanation offered — which is in the text, which is not embellished — is: you know how these people are. They said to me, make us a god. So I told them to bring their gold. They brought their gold. I threw it in the fire. And this calf came out.

The fire made the calf. That is the explanation.

Naomi Kessler — this time it's Aaron.

[narration]

Aaron appears in Exodus 4 as a solution to a problem Moses has. Moses, at the burning bush, has been given an assignment and objects to it on the grounds that he is not a good speaker — he uses a Hebrew phrase that is sometimes translated as slow of speech and tongue, the precise meaning debated, but the point is that Moses doesn't want to be the one talking. God's answer is to designate Aaron as his spokesman. Aaron is three years older than Moses. He meets him at the mountain of God and they return to Egypt together. From that point forward in the narrative, Moses is the one who hears from God, and Aaron is the one who says it to Pharaoh.

His role in what follows is consequential and consistent. He holds the staff that becomes a serpent before Pharaoh. He stretches it over the Nile and it turns to blood. He stretches it again and frogs cover the land. He strikes the dust and gnats appear. The early plagues, in Exodus, run through Aaron's hand as much as Moses's. He is a participant in the central events of the Exodus — not a bystander, not just a mouthpiece. He stands in Pharaoh's court alongside Moses and does the same work.

Then Moses goes up the mountain. He is gone for forty days. The people go to Aaron and say: make us a god who will go before us, because this Moses — we don't know what happened to him. And Aaron tells them to bring their gold earrings. They bring them. He takes them, fashions them with a tool, and makes a golden calf. Then he builds an altar in front of it and announces: tomorrow is a feast to the Lord. The people rise the next morning, offer burnt offerings, sit down to eat and drink, and rise to play.

When Moses comes down and confronts him, Aaron's explanation — the fire ate it and the calf came out — is, at minimum, an attempt to remove his own agency from the chain of events. He made it with a tool. The text says so, clearly, four verses earlier. The fire-ate-it account cannot be reconciled with the made-it-with-a-tool account. The tradition has noted this tension for a long time, and various explanations have been offered. What the text provides is the explanation Aaron gave.

[groan] Then: Leviticus 10. Aaron's sons Nadab and Abihu — who had been present at the covenant meal on the mountain with the elders of Israel, who are already ordained priests — offer what the text calls strange fire, unauthorized fire, before the Lord. Fire comes out from the presence of God and consumes them. They die immediately. Moses tells Aaron what God has said: I will be sanctified among those who are near me. And Aaron is silent. The Hebrew word used for his silence is a word that can mean grief, or shock, or acceptance — interpreters have read it in all three directions. Moses then tells Aaron and his remaining sons not to mourn — do not let your hair hang loose, do not tear your clothes, lest you die. The congregation will mourn, but not you.

The father of the dead men is not permitted to grieve them publicly. He stands in the door of the tent of meeting while the bodies of his sons are carried out by their cousins, and he says nothing the text records.

Aaron lives long enough to be present when Miriam is struck with a skin disease after she and Aaron speak against Moses — the text implies both were involved, Miriam is the one afflicted, and Aaron asks Moses to intervene, which Moses does. He lives long enough to be told, with Moses, at the waters of Meribah, that he will not enter the land of Canaan. He dies on Mount Hor, at the edge of it, after his priestly garments are transferred to his son Eleazar. The whole house of Israel mourns him for thirty days.

He was the voice assigned to a story that didn't belong to him, in a role he didn't choose, carrying weight the record has generally assigned to someone else. He also made the calf. Both of those things are true, and both are in the same chapters. If you want more of this — people the text gives to us whole, contradictions and all — that's what we're doing here.
