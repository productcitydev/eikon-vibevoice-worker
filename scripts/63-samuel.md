# Episode 63 — Samuel
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Format:** Character-centered, five-act depth; opens on the unanswered prayer that precedes his birth
**Hook variant:** skeleton #5 (Question → A → C), Slot A #4 / Slot C #4
**Thesis:** Samuel was the answer to a prayer that the religious establishment had already dismissed, and his entire career — from the night call at Shiloh to the anointing of a king he did not want Israel to have — ran on the tension between what God said and what Samuel would have chosen.
**Epistemic honesty:** The chronology of Samuel's judgeship and the transition to monarchy is debated; the text presents overlapping traditions without fully harmonizing them.

What does it do to a child — to grow up knowing you exist because someone else's grief was loud enough that God answered it? Not a casual prayer. A woman weeping in a temple so hard the priest thought she was drunk. She was not drunk. She was making a deal — if you give me a son, I will give him back. The son came. She gave him back. She walked him to Shiloh and left him there with the old priest whose own sons were a disgrace to the office. The child grew up in that building. Serving. Listening. Waiting for something he could not have known was coming.

I'm Naomi — that question, today, has a name: Samuel.

His mother Hannah brought him to Eli at the tabernacle in Shiloh when he was weaned. The text in 1 Samuel 2 records her prayer at the dedication — a song about reversal, about the powerful brought low and the lowly raised. She left. The boy stayed. He ministered before the LORD under Eli, in a time when the word of the LORD was rare and visions were infrequent. That is the text's own language. It sets the scene by telling you there was silence.

Then the night call. 1 Samuel 3. The boy is lying down in the temple, near the ark of God. A voice calls his name. He runs to Eli — here I am, you called me. Eli says: I did not call. Go back and lie down. It happens again. And again. Three times. Eli, old and nearly blind, finally understands what the boy does not: someone else is speaking.

[whispering]

Eli tells him: if you are called again, say — speak, LORD, for your servant is listening. The boy says it. And the message he receives is not gentle. God tells him that Eli's house is about to fall, that the judgment is coming and it will not be atoned for by sacrifice or offering. Samuel lies there until morning. He is afraid to tell Eli. Eli asks. Samuel tells him everything. Eli says: he is the LORD. Let him do what is good in his eyes.

That is how it begins. A child delivering judgment to the man who raised him.

Samuel grows. The text says the LORD was with him and let none of his words fall to the ground. He becomes judge over Israel — the last in the line before the monarchy. He circuits between Mizpah, Gilgal, Bethel, and his home at Ramah. He appoints his own sons as judges, but they take bribes and pervert justice. The elders come to him and say: you are old, your sons do not walk in your ways. Give us a king to judge us, like the other nations have.

The text says this displeased Samuel. God tells him: they have not rejected you — they have rejected me as their king. Warn them what a king will do. Samuel warns them. They insist. He anoints Saul.

Then it unravels. Saul disobeys. God tells Samuel he regrets making Saul king. Samuel grieves over Saul — the text says he cried out to the LORD all night. Then God tells him to stop grieving and go to Bethlehem. Fill your horn with oil. I am sending you to Jesse. One of his sons will be king. Samuel goes. He sees Jesse's sons. Seven pass before him. None is chosen. He asks: are these all your sons? There is one more — the youngest, out tending sheep. Bring him. David comes in. The LORD says: rise and anoint him. This is the one.

Samuel anoints David. Then he steps offstage. He dies in 1 Samuel 25 in a single sentence. All Israel gathered and mourned for him and buried him at his home in Ramah.

He appears once more. In 1 Samuel 28, Saul, desperate before a battle, visits a medium at Endor and asks her to bring up Samuel. She does. Samuel's shade says: why have you disturbed me? Tomorrow you and your sons will be with me.

A man called before he understood what calling meant. Given words he did not choose. Made to anoint what he did not want. The text does not record whether he was at peace with any of it. It records that he was faithful, and that faithfulness looked like grief as often as it looked like anything else.

That is how the record holds him. If you are new here — this is where we do that.
