A man lies on his left side for three hundred and ninety days, then turns to his right side for forty more. He is told to cook his food over a fire fueled in a way that makes it ritually unclean — and he objects, and is given a concession. He shaves his head and his beard with a sword, divides the hair into thirds, and burns one third, strikes one third with a blade, and scatters one third to the wind. He digs through a wall at night carrying his belongings on his shoulder like a refugee. All of this is commanded. All of it is public. He is a message performed in the flesh.

I'm Naomi Kessler. On this channel, we go looking for who people actually were, not just the stories about them. Today, we're looking at Ezekiel.

He was a priest. That is the first thing the text says about him — Ezekiel the priest, son of Buzi. He was trained for the temple. He knew the rituals, the boundaries between clean and unclean, the architecture of sacred space. And then Nebuchadnezzar came and took him to Babylon in the deportation of 597 BCE — the second deportation, documented independently in the Babylonian Chronicles. He was approximately twenty-five years old. He would never serve in the temple he was trained for. It was destroyed eleven years later.

Five years into exile, by the Kebar canal in Babylon, the heavens open and he sees the vision that begins the book: four living creatures, each with four faces, wheels within wheels covered in eyes, a vast expanse above them, and on a throne above the expanse, a figure like a man surrounded by radiance. He falls on his face. A voice speaks: Son of man, I am sending you.

The phrase "Son of man" appears over ninety times in the book. It is how God addresses Ezekiel — consistently, throughout — and it means simply "human being." The title is a reminder of what he is. The visions will be extraordinary. He is not.

[sigh]

What follows is a prophetic ministry unlike any other in the Hebrew Bible in its physical demands. The sign-acts are not metaphors described in speech. They are performances. God tells Ezekiel to build a model of Jerusalem under siege using a brick and an iron pan. To lie on his side for over a year, bearing the iniquity of Israel and Judah in days corresponding to years. To eat measured rations of bread and water on a schedule. Each act is a message to the exiles about what is coming — or what has already come — to the city they left behind.

Chapter 24 contains the most personally devastating command. God tells Ezekiel that his wife — described as "the delight of your eyes" — will die. At a stroke. And he is told: do not mourn. Do not weep. No tears. Bind your turban, put on your sandals, do not cover your lip, do not eat the bread of mourners. The text says his wife died in the evening, and in the morning he did as he was commanded.

The exiles ask him: will you not tell us what this means? He tells them: the temple — the delight of their eyes — will be taken, and their sons and daughters will fall by the sword, and they will do as he has done. They will not mourn publicly. Their grief will be silent.

His body is the message. His loss is the message. The distinction between the prophet and the prophecy collapses in Ezekiel more completely than in any other book.

And then, after the destruction — after the word comes in chapter 33 that the city has fallen — the visions shift. The valley of dry bones in chapter 37. Can these bones live? Breath enters them and they stand, a vast army. Restoration is possible. It is not a reversal of what was lost — it is something new built from what is dead.

Chapters 40 through 48 record a vision of a new temple, measured in extraordinary detail by a figure with a measuring rod. Ezekiel the priest, who never served in the original temple, is shown one he will never enter either. But he records every measurement. Every gate. Every room.

A priest without a temple. A husband without a wife. A man whose body was the sermon. The text does not say what he felt about any of it. It says what he did.

If you want the people behind the prophecies — the actual cost of carrying a message — that's what we do here. I'll see you in the next one.
