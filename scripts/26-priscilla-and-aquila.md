I'm Naomi, by the way. If you've been here before, you know this channel doesn't rank people by how much screen time the text gives them — it takes them seriously regardless. If you haven't been here before — same principle, different starting point. This episode belongs to Priscilla and Aquila.

They never preach a recorded sermon. They never write a letter that survives. They never perform a miracle the text names. And yet Paul calls them his co-workers in Christ Jesus — the same term he uses for Timothy, for Titus, for people he trusted with churches. They appear in three different cities across two decades of the early movement, always together, always working, always hosting. The record never mentions one without the other.

[narration]

They enter the text in Acts 18. Paul arrives in Corinth and finds them there — recently expelled from Rome under Claudius's edict, which Suetonius dates to approximately 49 CE. The Roman historian's account says Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome because of disturbances "at the instigation of Chrestus" — widely read by scholars as a reference to disputes about Christ within the Jewish community. Priscilla and Aquila were caught in that expulsion. They landed in Corinth. They were tentmakers — the same trade as Paul. He moved in with them.

That detail matters. Paul didn't just meet them; he lived and worked alongside them. The shared trade created daily proximity. Whatever theological formation happened between them occurred in the context of labor — cutting leather, stitching canvas, making a living. They worked together every day for eighteen months, the duration Acts gives for Paul's stay in Corinth.

When Paul sailed for Syria, they went with him — Acts 18:18. He left them in Ephesus. It's there that they heard Apollos in the synagogue, recognized what was incomplete in his teaching, and took him aside privately. Acts 18:26 gives Priscilla's name first: "Priscilla and Aquila... explained to him the way of God more accurately." The correction of one of the early movement's most gifted speakers was done by a married couple of tentmakers, at home, without public confrontation.

Romans 16:3–5, written later from Corinth, reveals more. Paul greets "Prisca and Aquila, my co-workers in Christ Jesus, who risked their necks for my life, to whom not only I give thanks but all the churches of the Gentiles." The phrase is stark — risked their necks. The Greek is literal: they laid down their own necks. When or how this happened, the text doesn't say. What it says is that all the Gentile churches knew about it and were grateful.

The same passage adds: "Greet also the church in their house." They hosted a congregation in their home. First Corinthians 16:19 says the same — "Aquila and Prisca, together with the church in their house, send you hearty greetings." They've moved back to Ephesus by this point. A house church, again.

[sigh]

Second Timothy 4:19 greets them one final time — they're apparently back in Ephesus again, or still there. Three cities across the record: Rome, Corinth, Ephesus. Expelled from one, established in two others, always building the same thing — a home where believers could gather, a workspace where theology got discussed over stitching, a partnership that every New Testament author who mentions them treats as a single unit.

The name-order question: of the six times they're named together, Priscilla comes first in four — Acts 18:18, 18:26, Romans 16:3, and 2 Timothy 4:19. In Greco-Roman convention, name order typically reflected social prominence or the person considered more notable. The majority scholarly reading is that Priscilla was the more publicly recognized of the two. The text doesn't explain why — it just consistently puts her name first and lets the convention speak for itself.

No sermons. No letters. No miracles. A couple who worked with their hands, opened their home, corrected a scholar without shaming him, risked their lives for Paul, and moved wherever the work needed them. The record names them together every time and never once records them apart. If you want more people the text preserved in that kind of quiet integrity — that's what we do here.
