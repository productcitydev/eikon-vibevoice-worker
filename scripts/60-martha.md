# Episode 60 — Martha

**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler (reverent-conviction register — warmth and gravity, not hype-speed)
**Format:** character-centered, full five-act depth. Opens on the line everyone misremembers — "Lord, do you not care?" — and reframes it: Martha is not the cautionary tale about busyness that most sermons make her. She is the woman who made one of the most precise theological statements in the Gospels, unprompted, four days after her brother's death.

"Lord, do you not care that my sister has left me to serve alone?" That is the line people remember. It has become shorthand for a sermon: don't be like Martha, be like Mary. Sit at Jesus's feet. Don't fuss over the kitchen. The spiritual is better than the practical.

But that reading requires ignoring almost everything else the text says about this woman. Because the next time she appears — in a completely different Gospel, years later in the narrative — she says something so theologically precise that if Peter had said it, it would be in every catechism on earth.

I'm Naomi. We tell the stories the way the text actually gives them. This one is Martha.

Here is what Luke 10 actually says. Jesus enters a village. A woman named Martha "received him into her house." She is the host. She is named first. Her sister Mary sits at Jesus's feet listening. Martha — distracted with much serving — asks Jesus to tell Mary to help. Jesus responds: "Martha, Martha, you are anxious and troubled about many things, but one thing is necessary. Mary has chosen the good portion."

Notice what Jesus does not say. He does not say Martha's work is wrong. He does not say serving is sinful. He says Mary's choice will not be taken from her. The "one thing necessary" is not a rebuke of action — it is a statement about priority in that specific moment. The tradition turned it into a binary. The text did not.

Then John 11. Lazarus — Martha's brother — is dead. Four days dead. Jesus arrives late, and Martha is the one who goes out to meet him. Mary stays in the house. Martha speaks first, and what she says is extraordinary: "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died. But even now I know that whatever you ask from God, God will give you."

[surprised]

That is not a complaint. That is a theological statement wrapped in grief. If you had been here — acknowledging his power. But even now — asserting that death is not the boundary of that power. She is reasoning in real time, under pressure, through tears, and arriving at a conclusion most of the disciples never articulate this clearly.

Jesus says: "Your brother will rise again." Martha responds with the standard eschatological answer: "I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day." She knows the theology. She has it filed correctly. And then Jesus says: "I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live. Do you believe this?"

Her answer: "Yes, Lord; I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God, who is coming into the world."

That is John 11:27. It is a christological confession. It uses three titles — Christ, Son of God, the Coming One — each drawn from a distinct strand of messianic expectation. Peter's confession in Matthew 16 is celebrated as the rock on which the church is built. Martha's confession, equally precise, equally unsolicited, spoken while her brother's body is decomposing in a tomb, is rarely quoted in the same breath.

She then goes back to the house, calls Mary quietly, and Mary goes to Jesus. Martha has already said what needed to be said. She has already believed what needed to be believed. The miracle at the tomb — "Lazarus, come out" — follows her confession, not the other way around. She believed before she saw.

One chapter later, John 12: a supper is given for Jesus in Bethany. Mary anoints his feet with costly perfume. And the text says, plainly, "Martha served." No rebuke this time. No correction. She is doing exactly what she did before — providing, hosting, making the space work — and the text offers no commentary. Because the text never condemned her work. It told her, once, that in one moment, listening came first. She heard it. And then she went on being Martha.

A theologian in the kitchen. A confessor beside a tomb. The text knew who she was even when the sermons forgot.
