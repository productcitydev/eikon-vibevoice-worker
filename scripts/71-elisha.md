# Episode 71 — Elisha

**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler (reverent-conviction register — warmth and gravity, not hype-speed)
**Format:** character-centered, full five-act depth. Opens on the most unsettling incident in his record — the bears — before pulling back to reveal the full prophetic career that framed it, and why the text records such disparate acts under one name.
**Hook variant:** skeleton #5 (Question→A→C), Slot A #9 / Slot C #6
**Thesis:** Elisha's prophetic career spans more recorded miracles than any other Old Testament prophet — acts of extraordinary tenderness alongside acts of extraordinary severity — and the text presents both without apology, as if it does not consider them contradictory.
**Epistemic honesty note:** the episode names, directly, that the Elisha cycle in 2 Kings reads as a collection of episodic narratives rather than a continuous biography, and that historical corroboration for his specific acts is absent outside the biblical text itself, though the geopolitical context (Aramean wars, the Jehu dynasty) is independently attested.

What do you do with a prophet who raises a dead child to life in one chapter and calls bears down on teenagers in another? How do you reconcile a man who purifies a poisoned pot of stew to feed the hungry with a man who strikes an entire army blind? Most people who know Elisha's name know one of those stories — usually the bears — and stop there, either because it disturbs them or because it confirms something they already wanted to believe. The full record is stranger than either reaction accounts for. It is also larger.

It's Naomi. Today's name is Elisha.

He enters the narrative in 1 Kings 19, when Elijah — mid-flight from Jezebel, freshly told that his work is not finished — walks past a field where Elisha is plowing with twelve yoke of oxen. Elijah throws his mantle over him and keeps walking. Elisha leaves the oxen, runs after him, asks to kiss his parents goodbye, then slaughters the oxen, burns the plowing equipment as fuel to cook the meat, gives it to the people around him, and follows. He burns his livelihood. The text gives this two verses. No hesitation is recorded.

He serves Elijah for an unspecified period. When the day comes for Elijah to be taken up, Elisha refuses to leave his side — three times told to stay behind, three times he insists on following. He asks for a double portion of Elijah's spirit. He watches the chariot of fire take Elijah away. He picks up the fallen mantle. He strikes the Jordan and it parts.

What follows is the most concentrated miracle cycle in the Old Testament. He purifies bad water with salt. He multiplies a widow's oil to pay her debts. He promises a childless Shunammite woman a son — and when that son dies years later, he goes to the child, lies on top of him, and the boy revives. He feeds a hundred men with twenty loaves. He heals Naaman's leprosy by telling him to wash in the Jordan seven times — and then curses his own servant Gehazi with that same leprosy for taking payment Elisha had refused.

During the Aramean wars, the record becomes geopolitical. The king of Aram is furious that his military plans keep being intercepted. His officers tell him: Elisha tells the king of Israel the very words you speak in your bedroom. An army is sent to capture one prophet. Elisha's servant panics at the sight of the surrounding force. Elisha prays: open his eyes. The servant sees the hills full of horses and chariots of fire. Elisha then prays the army blind, leads them into Samaria, and tells the king of Israel to feed them and send them home. The raids stop.

Later, during a siege so severe that women are eating their own children, the king of Israel blames Elisha and sends a messenger to kill him. Elisha prophesies that by the next day food will be absurdly cheap. An officer doubts him out loud: even if God made windows in heaven, could this happen? Elisha tells the officer he will see it but not eat of it. The next day, the Arameans flee in panic. The officer is trampled in the gate during the rush for food. Both halves of the prophecy come true.

The bears. 2 Kings 2:23-24. Youths from Bethel mock him: go up, you baldhead. He curses them in the name of the LORD. Two bears come out of the woods and maul forty-two of them. The text records this without commentary, without apology, without explanation. It sits in the same collection as the raised child and the fed multitude.

The text does not smooth these together into a portrait that is comfortable to hold. It records a man through whom extraordinary power moved in both directions — mercy and severity, provision and judgment — and presents both as legitimate expressions of the same prophetic office. Whether that composite portrait is coherent depends on what you think a prophet is for.

Elisha dies in 2 Kings 13. Even his bones carry residual power: a dead man thrown into his grave revives on contact with them. The text records that detail and moves on, as though it is simply what happens when this kind of life leaves a mark on the physical world.

The record doesn't ask you to be comfortable with him. It asks you to account for the whole of him. That's harder.

If you want more of that — the full person, not the edited version — that's what we do here. See you next time.
