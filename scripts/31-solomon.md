# Episode 31 — Solomon
**Narrator:** Naomi Kessler
**Hook variant:** skeleton #10 (Stakes→A→C), Slot A #6 / Slot C #9
**Thesis:** Solomon asked for wisdom, received it, and then systematically violated every specific warning given to kings in Deuteronomy 17 — horses, wives, gold — as though wisdom and obedience were separate categories entirely.
**Grounded in:** 1 Kings 1–11, 2 Chronicles 1–9, Deuteronomy 17:14–20
**Tags used:** [dramatic]

---

Deuteronomy 17 contains a short passage about what a future king of Israel must not do. It is specific. The king must not acquire many horses. He must not acquire many wives, lest his heart turn away. He must not acquire excessive silver and gold. Three prohibitions — horses, wives, gold — each one named, each one clear.

Solomon acquired fourteen hundred chariots and twelve thousand horses. He married seven hundred wives of royal birth and had three hundred concubines. The weight of gold that came to him yearly was six hundred sixty-six talents. The text records these numbers without editorial. It records them in a book that also records the moment God offered Solomon anything he wanted, and Solomon asked for wisdom, and God was pleased.

How both of those things can be true about the same person is not a paradox the text resolves.

This is Naomi. Solomon.

[narration]

Solomon is born to David and Bathsheba — not their first child together. Their first child, conceived in the affair while Uriah was alive, died seven days after birth. Solomon comes after. His birth is noted in 2 Samuel 12:24 alongside an unusual detail: the LORD loved him. A prophet — Nathan, the same one who confronted David about Bathsheba — sends word and gives the child a second name: Jedidiah, "beloved of the LORD." The child of the scandal, after the death, carries divine favor from the start.

His accession to the throne is not clean. First Kings 1 describes a succession crisis — Adonijah, David's older surviving son, declares himself king while David is still alive. Bathsheba and Nathan intervene. David designates Solomon. Solomon is anointed while Adonijah is still at his feast. Once secure on the throne, Solomon systematically eliminates threats: Adonijah is executed after requesting Abishag. Joab is killed at the altar. Shimei is placed under house arrest and later executed for violating its terms. The kingdom is established in Solomon's hand. The word the text uses is "established." It took blood to get there.

Then Gibeon. First Kings 3. God appears to Solomon in a dream and says: ask what I shall give you. Solomon's request is famous — he asks for an understanding heart to judge the people, to discern between good and evil. He does not ask for long life, or riches, or the death of his enemies. God is pleased. He gives Solomon wisdom, and also the things he did not ask for — riches and honor — so that no king in his lifetime will compare with him.

[dramatic] What follows is a reign that the text describes as the apex of Israelite civilization. The temple is built — seven years of construction, the materials alone staggering in scale. The palace takes thirteen years. The visit of the Queen of Sheba in chapter 10 serves as an outside witness: she comes with hard questions and leaves saying the half was not told to her. The administrative structure, the building projects, the trade networks — first Kings 9 and 10 read like the description of an empire functioning at its theoretical maximum.

And then chapter 11. "King Solomon loved many foreign women." The verse names them — Moabites, Ammonites, Edomites, Sidonians, Hittites — from nations about which God had said you shall not intermarry with them, for surely they will turn your heart after their gods. Solomon clung to them in love. His wives turned his heart after other gods, and his heart was not wholly true to the LORD his God, as was the heart of David his father. He built high places for Chemosh and for Molech. The man who built the temple built altars to other gods.

God speaks to Solomon a final time. Because you have done this, the kingdom will be torn from you — though not in your lifetime, for the sake of David. One tribe will remain. The rest will be taken.

The text does not psychologize the collapse. It does not explain how a man with divinely granted wisdom could walk straight into the three prohibitions that were written down before he was born. It records what he asked for. It records what he did. The proverbs carry his name. The high places also carry his name. Rehoboam inherits a kingdom already sentenced, and the split comes within days of Solomon's death.

Wisdom was given. Obedience was not guaranteed by it. The text holds both without resolving the distance between them. I'll see you in the next one.
